How Are We Doing
During the 2025 reporting period, 25 percent of the 342 targeted high priority watersheds had flows added for fish. The percentage is similar to the prior four reporting years. The amount of water
placed instream can fluctuate from year-to-year based on water user interest in leasing water instream and the location of water rights proposed for instream lease. Since this KPM was created in
2002, the Department has permanently protected a total of 1561.788 cubic feet per second (cfs) water instream. This total is comprised of the following: 1) instream transfers at 428.12 cfs; 2)
allocations of conserved water at 268.268 cfs; and 3) converted hydroelectric rights at 865.4 cfs. In 2024, an additional 4935.24 cfs was protected instream through temporary instream leases, with
3871.30 cfs protected from leases involved with water rights for hydroelectric power generation.
Factors Affecting Results
The 2025 reporting period was the calendar year 2024.  Streamflow restoration efforts depend on the voluntary actions of water right holders to place water instream. Success on this measure relies
on the hard work of our conservation partners, our staff, and a general increased comfort level with dedicating water instream among water users. Streamflow restoration benefits from well-
established, active conservation partners with 33 percent of Oregon's flow restoration transactions involving a third party such as The Freshwater Trust, Deschutes River Conservancy, or Trout
Unlimited, while the remaining 67 percent of flow restoration activities occur directly between the water right holder and the Department.

How Are We Doing
In calendar year 2024, staff reported a total of 8,928 regulatory actions. Regulatory actions are actions by staff that cause a change in water use behavior. Of the 8,928 total regulatory actions taken in
2024, 3,037 or 34 percent of the total, were conducted to regulate for instream water rights. Reported regulatory actions are taken to protect senior instream water rights rose from 2,941 in 2023 to
3,041 in 2024. While the 2024 target was not met it was due in large part to not needing to regulate for some instream water rights as water conditions, including precipitation, were more favorable to
the established instream water rights.
Watermasters and assistant watermasters are responsible for monitoring and protecting instream water rights. These staff members are also assisted by regional hydrographic technicians who
conduct numerous streamflow measurements in support of instream water right monitoring. Watermasters report monitoring activities and regulatory actions taken each calendar year for each stream
into the Field Activity Database (FAD) for ongoing tracking and enforcement purposes.
Factors Affecting Results
This KPM is specific to a regulation on behalf of instream water rights. Instream water rights are often junior to other surface water rights and are actively monitored by the Department. Many stream
flows with instream water rights are met naturally throughout the season which do not require regulation on their behalf. In years with well-timed precipitation and high stream flows, the total number of
streams regulated is likely to go down. In years with lower stream flows the total number of streams regulated is likely to go up because of the greater demand and less water supply for all beneficial
uses. The number of streams regulated varies with the amount and timing of rainfall and snowmelt run off, temperatures, as well as the availability of staff resources. In addition, actions taken by field
staff help to address unauthorized uses of water benefit instream needs even if not fully reflected by this particular metric.

How Are We Doing
In 2024, watermasters and assistant watermasters reported 8,928 regulatory actions. Field staff also reported 29,605 compliance checks across the state to determine if water right holders were
complying with the law or a regulatory action. Of the 29,605 compliance checks, 96.4 percent of the time water use was consistent with existing regulatory orders or Oregon water laws. If non-
compliance is observed, in many instances, compliance can be achieved immediately. Watermaster staff can decrease or stop non-compliant water use by closing the head gate and posting it with a
yellow tag or in the near term by communicating with the water user. In other instances, non-compliance may result in issuance of a Notice of Violation or Notice of Assessment. With approximately
90,500 water rights statewide and several hundred thousand exempt use wells, plus an unknown number of unauthorized uses, it is not possible for Department staff to fully assess the compliance of
every use within the state each year.
Factors Affecting Results
As the Field Services Division has grown in recent years due to legislative investments, the ability to address reported issues and complaints in a timely fashion has improved. The investments have
allowed the Department to be more forward-looking and proactively investigate water use via staff-initiated investigations. This is reflected in the number of community-based complaints continuing a
downward trend of 242, which is down from the previous years of 377 in 2023, 508 in 2022, and from 1,120 in 2021. In 2024, the number of field staff-initiated investigations declined to 727 after
increases in previous years (1,142 in 2023, 894 in 2022, and 732 in 2021). This decrease in staff-initiated investigations can be directly attributed to staff capacity resulting from promotions, departures
from the agency, and staff being out on extended leave. Despite a temporary decrease in capacity in 2024, the number of compliance checks reported in 2024 (29,605) is on par with those reported in
2023 (29,304).
As part of the regulatory actions, 102 notices of violation (NOV) were issued in calendar year 2024. This is a decrease from the previous year in which the Department issued 162 NOVs. Both the
regulated and unregulated cannabis industry accounts for a large number of the sites where NOV’s are issued. The decrease in the number of NOVs can be attributed to a decrease in the cannabis
industry and changes in statute to clarify how to legally use hauled water (HB 4061). The formation of the Enforcement Section (ES) in 2022 resulted in the streamlining of enforcement processes. The
new section continues to assist in enforcement efficiencies to include better track the of enforcement referrals leading to issuance of orders and regulatory notices. In addition to the above NOV totals,
the ES assisted field staff with the issuance of 75 measurement device notices and issued 13 notices of assessment for civil penalties totaling over $86,000. The Department may not collect all of
these assessments as some may be reduced as part of stipulated settlement agreements, or they remain part of ongoing casework. The increased investments in field and enforcement staff are
improving water use compliance across the state.

How Are We Doing
During the current reporting cycle, Department staff routinely monitored 434 wells in the State Observation Well Network, compared to 420 in the 2024 report. This is well above the current target of 15
percent increase in the number of wells from 2001 and continues to improve. Wells in the Observation Well Network produce long standing and high-quality aquifer water level data across the state
and are meaningful to the Department’s work.
Although not included in the metric, the Department also measures or receives data from 2,893 additional “observation wells.” The collected data are used to regularly monitor groundwater levels
statewide, for special projects, and in administrative areas, and serve as measurements to satisfy groundwater permit conditions for water right holders. Additional observation wells include 837 wells
that were measured 1,634 times by Department staff. Of those wells, 176 are equipped with automated data-logging pressure transducers that collect water level data several times per day and
produce high-frequency time-series data sets. Additionally, the Department receives data from licensed professionals who collected 2,484 water level measurements from another 1,993 wells as
required by various water right permit conditions. The Department archives and provides these and other groundwater related data via the Groundwater Information System database and web
interface, providing this information directly to public.
Factors Affecting Results
The percentage of monitored wells has increased as the Department added new wells to the State Observation Well Network while replacing others that had previously been removed from the
network. Since 2013, the Legislature included dedicated funding for observation wells in the Department's budget, though the total budget has varied from biennium to biennium. This funding is used
for a combination of drilling new wells and maintaining access to existing wells by paying for measurement tubes and other related alterations. In the 2023-2025 biennium, OWRD completed nine
access alterations and drilled five new observation wells. Apart from the 29 new observation wells added by the Department in the last decade, State Observation Wells are largely owned by private
and other public entities. This can create a long-term access issue to wells, as the Department must rely on private well owners for continued participation in the network and the ongoing reliability and
maintenance provided by those outside parties. When the Department loses access to an observation well, the Department tries to find or drill a suitable replacement well in the same general area
targeting the same aquifer, but this process takes time, staff and fiscal resources. As a result, the number and location of State Observation Wells vary from year to year.

How Are We Doing
In 2000, 2,385 Significant Point of Diversion (SigPODs) were identified as strategic points for measurement. Working directly with landowners, there have been 1,124 measuring devices installed on
SigPODs across the state. One additional device was installed in the 2024 calendar year through the coordinated efforts of watermasters and assistant watermasters in the Field Services Division
(FSD). In addition to the measurement devices installed on SigPODs, staff have evaluated in the field another 709 significant diversions that are currently not in use. This number changes as a water
user may go several years without using water and then irrigate for a season to reportedly protect the water right from forfeiture. Initiating use would require the installation of a measuring device.
Approximately 481 of the original 2,385 significant diversions still need measuring devices installed.
Factors Affecting Results
The timing of data submittal and the data entry by staff may result in small changes from year-to-year on previously reported numbers, not reflected in these reports. This KPM does not account for
measuring devices installed because of other Department actions. The Department focuses measuring device installation where there is noncompliance, complaints, or suspicion of unauthorized use,
or in the case of groundwater, where the Department is conducting scientific research and measurement is needed. This KPM does not track and report on that work, as most of those points of
diversion are not deemed significant points of diversion.
While the FSD has experienced an increase in staff over the past several years, the division has seen staff turnover, particularly for assistant watermasters. Assistant watermasters are often used for
developmental opportunities where staff promote internally or to other agencies after gaining valuable field experience. Capacity in the field has been varied due to promotions, extended leave, and
departures from the agency, however, compliance checks have not decreased. The agency must respond to suspicions of and complaints from the public related unauthorized water use, as well as
ongoing drought and subsequent regulation which all increase regulatory workloads on staff; this limits progress on this KPM. The installation of measuring devices typically occurs outside the
irrigation season and is weather dependent, particularly in freezing conditions. Targeted outreach and education are needed to help landowners with measuring device installations. Success with
measuring device installation is directly related to time spent by Department field staff, primarily watermasters and assistant watermasters, working with landowners and consultants.

Between July 1, 2024, and June 30, 2025, the Department received 23 Water Management and Conservation Plans (WMCPs) and reviewed 100 percent within the 90-day goal. This continues the

The number of plans received this year increased from 16 in the previous year to 23. Compliance continues to improve among water suppliers required to submit two- and five-year progress reports

direct assistance to entities developing new or updated WMCPs. These efforts reflect the Department’s ongoing commitment to improving water management and conservation throughout Oregon.

How Are We Doing
The Department issued an Initial Review within 45 days of application filing for 43 percent of applicable water right applications within this reporting period. This is an eight-percentage point increase
over the previous period. In addition, the Department issued nearly six times more Initial Reviews for this reporting period compared to the prior reporting period.

Department can implement new process improvements resulting from the 2025 legislation and the agency’s other process improvement efforts, the Department anticipates reduced processing times
and improved performance on this measure.

How Are We Doing
A total of 253 transfer final orders were issued during the reporting period, 27 of which were issued within 120 days of the transfer application being filed. Unfortunately, at the beginning of this
reporting period, the Transfer Program Advisor retired, which is a critical position for completing transfers. Despite this, the agency has reduced the transfers backlog; the number of pending
applications at the end of the previous reporting period (June 30, 2024) was 497, compared to 446 pending applications at the end of this reporting period (June 30, 2025). The Department’s goal is to
reduce the number of pending applications to 250 or less, at which point staff will be better able to take on processing of new applications as soon as they are filed. The Department expects activity
with water right transfers to increase over time as users seek changes and more flexibility with their rights.
Factors Affecting Results
The Transfer Section consists of six caseworkers and one senior level Program Advisor to provide training and guidance to caseworkers and provide quality assurance reviews on final transfer
documents. Two years ago, the Department learned that it is difficult for the Transfer Program Advisor to keep up with the pace of production for all six caseworkers, leading to a bottleneck of
documents awaiting final review. Two additional senior level staff were redirected to help perform final reviews, resulting in bottleneck reduction and quicker turnaround times. Temporary transfer
applications, district temporary transfer applications, and drought transfer applications can be processed more quickly than standard permanent transfer applications, instream transfers, and
groundwater registration modifications. Standard permanent transfer application review processes require 3 formal documents (issuance of draft preliminary determinations (DPD), preliminary
determinations (PD), and final orders (FO)) as compared to the temporary and drought transfer processes, which only require issuance of the FO. Standard permanent transfer process and waiting
times required in rule and statute total 105 days – this figure does not factor in any time necessary for evaluating an application, awaiting completion of injury reviews from the watermaster and
Groundwater Section hydrogeologists, preparing the required documents (DPD, PD, public notice, and FO), completing data entry and other administrative processing tasks, or awaiting responses
from the applicant. It is difficult for staff to meet this KPM when processing standard permanent transfer applications. In addition, there is continued utilization of the District Temporary Transfer Pilot
Project, which does not require processing of the transfers by Salem staff and significantly reduced the number of easy-to-process temporary district transfer applications, further limiting the
Department’s ability to meet this KPM. Further, in many parts of the state, significant time is required by the Department’s hydrogeologist staff to review groundwater transfer applications because of
the technical nature of groundwater injury reviews.
In 2025, the Legislature passed several pieces of legislation, in coordination with the Department, with the goal to streamline the water right transfer processes. Applications covered by this reporting
period were not subject to these new streamlining provisions. The Department is working to implement the 2025 legislation, along with other process improvements, by April 1, 2026. Further the
Department has hired a contractor to work with staff over the next year to conduct a Lean/Six-Sigma review of some of the Transfer Program processes. For the next year, the Department anticipates
a temporary slowdown in application processing while staff are participating in the rulemaking, working with a contractor to analyze our processes, and working to implement the new rules and process
improvements. Once the new Transfer Program Advisor is hired and the Department implements improvement efforts, the Department anticipates reduced processing times and improved
performance on this measure.

How Are We Doing
The percent of water users submitting water-use reports as required for the 2025 reporting period was 78 percent; no change from the previous reporting period. In the past five reporting periods, this
KPM has stayed steady or decreased. The total number of reporters required to submit reports is 2,876 statewide. During this period, the Water Use Reporting coordinator sent out annual reminder
letters of the reporting requirement, sent out reminder emails and postcards, entered data submitted via paper forms, and provided phone and email customer support to the internal and external
customers.
Factors Affecting Results
Over the past five years, the metric has remained steady. In response, the Department is evaluating what additional actions are needed to reach the target performance level. One contributing factor
may be staffing limitations as only one person staffs this program and capacity for additional proactive outreach to individuals or entities not in compliance is limited.
Department staff have identified two major challenges that reporters often face: (1) a lack of resources to collect or report data, and (2) insufficient knowledge of reporting requirements. Some water
right holders lack the necessary equipment or staff time, while others experience lapses in compliance due to knowledge not being transferred during personnel changes.
Currently, about 22 percent of required reporters are out of compliance. Of these, 38 percent are federal entities, 28 percent are non-governmental organizations, 20 percent are irrigation or special
districts, 12 percent are cities or counties, and two percent are state entities. It is possible that each of these groups requires different types of support to improve compliance.
