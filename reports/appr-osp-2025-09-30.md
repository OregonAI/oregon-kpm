How Are We Doing
Historical Context: The previous metric for this Key Performance Metric (KPM) was to reduce fatal crashes on state and interstate highways where OSP has primary responsibility by 2.5% annually
(from 2012 to 2022), reaching a target of 101 fatal crashes by 2022. Due to factors beyond OSP's control, such as the licensed number of drivers and vehicle miles traveled, it was unlikely that OSP
would be able to achieve a purely nominal target.
Instead, OSP changed the metric based on the number of fatal crashes in vehicle miles traveled (VMT) on highway segments where Oregon State Police has primary responsibility. In 2015, the fatal
crash rate per 1,000,000,000 VMT was 7.79. Between 2010 - 2015, the lowest fatal crash rate per 1,000,000,000 VMT was 5.63 (in 2013). Using an average of 100 fatal crashes per 1,000,000,000
VMT over six years (2010 - 2015), OSP's new target is a fatal crash rate of 5.06 or lower per 1,000,000,000 VMT.
OSP investigated 212 fatal crashes in 2024 on OSP patrolled highways, a 20% decrease from the previous calendar year. State Highway VMT data is not yet published for 2024, so this update will
use 2023 data (21.962 million VMT).  ODOT’s data would calculate the fatal crash rate at 9.65 for the 2024 calendar year.
Data for this KPM is sourced from the ODOT Crash Analysis & Reporting Unit's Crash Data Viewer, and ODOT’s Initial Fatal Crash Information Viewer online dashboards. Data is preliminary and
subject to change until official crash data summaries are published.
Factors Affecting Results
Sworn vacancies in the Patrol Services Division in nearly every patrol office impact our ability to provide visible deterrence, education, and enforcement of traffic violations frequently attributed to
collisions. If the trend of improved recruiting for new Troopers continues, we expect improvement in visible patrols and proactive engagement around traffic safety issues.
Within OSP's control, we emphasize enforcement priorities for existing staff and public messaging about driving behaviors most often contributing to serious injury and fatal crashes (SOLID:

experiencing significant traffic safety issues.
Patrol offices around the state collaborate with ODOT and other partners on engineering and education efforts to reduce crashes. Troopers continue to receive enhanced training in rendering medical
aid, including the recognition and treatment of life-threatening trauma. Factors outside OSP control include economic conditions contributing to changes in traffic volume/patterns, adverse weather
events, the number of active drivers on roadways, and annual vehicle miles traveled.

How Are We Doing
OSP clearance rates for the last five years have been revised downward to reflect data corrections. OSP recently communicated with ODOT to ensure OSP patrol coverage on interstate and highway
segments was accurate in their analytics. It appears some highway segments were previously excluded from the data set, which may have contributed to the reported clearance rate skewing higher.
The past five years now shows a clearance rate ranging from 70-74%.
OSP clearance rates for statewide lane-blocking crashes in 2024 held steady at 70% compared to recent years. Incomplete 2025 data shows a slight improvement year to date. (Source: ODOT Traffic
Incident Management website - OSP Clearance dashboard)
Factors Affecting Results
Staffing shortages affecting nearly every OSP patrol office and worksite impact our ability to respond to lane-blocking crashes rapidly and consistently. Patrol Division staffing continues to improve into
2025, but there is a delay between hiring and fielding new Troopers on the highway due to training requirements. The rural nature of Eastern Oregon contributes to longer response times for first
responders, including law enforcement, emergency medical, ODOT Incident Response or maintenance staff, and tow companies. In 2024, only 30% of crashes investigated by OSP were east of the
Cascades but were overrepresented in those incidents with lane closures exceeding 90 minutes (44%).  (Source: ODOT Traffic Incident Management website - OSP Clearance dashboard)
Traffic Incident Management (TIM) classes are regularly provided at the Oregon Public Safety Academy, which introduces these concepts to new officers (including all Troopers) early in their careers.
OSP Troopers also teach TIM classes to first responders around the state to enhance safe and efficient response to roadway-blocking incidents. Advanced collision reconstruction technology and
growth in investigative competencies have generally reduced on-scene investigation time, but recent case law has led to longer time on scene to secure vehicles in criminal crash investigations.

How Are We Doing
In 2023 (reported in 2025) we exceeded the target and resolved 96% of Major Crime Team investigations within 12 months.
Every year since 2014, the 93% resolution rate target (established in 2009) had been met or exceeded. For 2018 (reported in 2020), the target was raised to a 95% resolution rate. Fortunately, the
Major Crime Section detectives achieved a 96% resolution rate in 2018, which exceeded the new target. The resolution rate for 2019 (reported in 2021) climbed to 99%. For 2020 (reported in 2022),
the resolution rate was 97%, once again exceeding the target. In 2021 we hit 98% resolution rate, continuing to exceed the target rate. In 2022 (reported in 2024) we missed the target by a small
margin (3%), receiving a 92% resolution rate. Of the 83 cases Major Crimes detectives investigated in 2022 only 7 remained open past 12 months, which represents only 8% of those cases.
Factors Affecting Results
Major crime investigations are inherently complex and require a coordinated, resource-intensive approach. Our CID Detectives consistently rise to this challenge, balancing their investigative
responsibilities with a wide range of specialized assignments, including SWAT, Crisis Response Team (CRT), and Mobile Response Team (MRT) deployments. These roles often place them at the
forefront of critical incidents and civil disturbances, where their expertise directly supports community safety and life-saving operations.  While these high-priority assignments may occasionally impact
the pace of investigations, they also reflect the versatility and commitment of our detectives. Major crimes are typically handled by a team, and even when one member is temporarily reassigned, the
collaborative structure ensures continuity. Despite demanding caseloads, ongoing training, and administrative duties, our teams remain focused on delivering thorough, high-quality investigations.
These factors, while influencing performance metrics, also highlight the depth of responsibility our detectives carry and the exceptional service they provide across multiple fronts.

How Are We Doing
In 2024 (reported in 2025), the Oregon State Police Drug Enforcement Section (DES) dismantled or disrupted 82 Drug Trafficking Organizations (DTOs)—exceeding the Key Performance Measure
(KPM) target of 45 by 37. This marks the highest total in the past five years and reflects a continued upward trend from the numbers reported in 2022 (13 DTOs), 2023 (39 DTOs), and 2024 (44
DTOs).
This success is attributed to DES’s strategic realignment of resources, a sharpened focus on fentanyl and methamphetamine, and improvements in data collection methodology. The last time the KPM
was exceeded to this degree was in 2019, when 73 DTOs were dismantled or disrupted. The 2025 results not only surpass that benchmark but also demonstrate a sustained recovery from the lower
numbers seen during the COVID-19 pandemic and the early impacts of Measure 110.
Factors Affecting Results
In alignment with the agency’s mission to preserve life, the Drug Enforcement Section (DES) continues to refine its strategy, placing a strong emphasis on interdicting methamphetamine and fentanyl
—the substances most responsible for Oregon’s ongoing overdose epidemic. According to preliminary data from the Oregon Health Authority, 1,832 Oregonians died from drug overdoses in 2023,
with methamphetamine and fentanyl identified as the primary contributors.
To address this crisis, DES implemented several key changes. In 2023, detectives were reassigned from marijuana investigations to local task forces and the Domestic Highway Enforcement (DHE)
team. This strategic realignment led to a 152% increase in methamphetamine seizures, rising from 785 pounds in 2023 to 1,982 pounds in 2024. Fentanyl seizures, measured in dosage units due to
the drug’s high potency, also increased significantly—by 32%—from 1.8 million in 2023 to nearly 2.4 million in 2024. Meanwhile, heroin seizures continued their downward trend, with only 25 pounds
seized in 2024, representing a 78% decrease from the 116 pounds seized in 2020.
OSP also continues to maintain a strong presence on multi-jurisdictional drug task forces, particularly those focused on mid- to upper-level DTOs. These investigations are often complex and time-
intensive but yield more impactful and lasting results. Additionally, while DES supported nine missions in Downtown Portland during 2023 and 2024 to address public drug use and overdose concerns,
fewer such missions were conducted in 2025. This allowed detectives to redirect their efforts toward long-term DTO investigations, contributing to the record number of dismantled or disrupted
organizations.

How Are We Doing
In the 2024 calendar year (2025 report year), the percentage of analytical requests completed in 30 days or less was 20%, which was an increase from the previous year (19%). It should also be noted
that the percentage of requests completed within 90 days improved from 54% to 61%.
Individual discipline KPMs for Blood Alcohol (sub-discipline of Toxicology), Field Investigations, and Biology were the highest in the Division, all over 30%. The largest year over year KPM increases
were in Biology (33% to 46%), Blood Alcohol (31% to 38%) and Toxicology (2% to 8%).
The most significant decrease in individual discipline KPMs was in Drug Chemistry, from 53% to 24%.
Although casework production increased by 12% in 2024 as compared to 2023, it did not keep up with demand. The total number of requests received by the FSD in 2024 was up 20% from the prior
year.
The Forensic Services Division (FSD) previously requested a resetting of KPM goals beginning in 2023, with a 4% increase every year until reaching 80% in 2037.
Factors Affecting Results
Multiple factors have impacted this KPM.
The passage of HB4002 significantly increased the number of drug chemistry submissions coming into the laboratory system. Total submissions were up 43% in 2024 even though HB4002 only went
into effect the last quarter of the year.
The volume of Toxicology work as a percent of the Division total increased from 54% to 59% in 2024, which has an outsized impact to the total KPM. The Toxicology KPM increased from 3% to 8%
during this same time period.
Although the new drugs in blood toxicology workflows are 2-3 times faster than previous methods, the Division has not seen a corresponding drop in requests for drugs in urine analysis. Additionally,
the FSD is seeing 3-4 times more postmortem toxicology submissions than anticipated based on the best data available at the time. Postmortem toxicology submissions increased by 84% in 2024 and
antemortem toxicology requests increased by 10%.
To keep up with the increasing number and complexity of discovery and public record requests, the FSD has had to shift resources into that work unit. The FSD responded to 568 requests in 2024,
which was more than double the 264 requests received in 2023. Although the bulk of this work is performed by managerial and support staff, certain aspects involve Forensic Scientists, which takes
them away from casework.
Manager Comments:
The passage of HB4002 included position authority and funding for two new support staff and four new Drug Chemists. The new two support staff are fully trained and are currently assisting with
multiple aspects of Drug Chemistry. All four Drug Chemists have been hired and are in various stages of training. Three of them are projected to be fully authorized to perform this work in Q4 of 2025
and the last should complete her training in Q2 of 2026. This is in addition to two other scientists who are also expected to be fully authorized in Drug Chemistry by the end of 2025. The extra capacity
should help address the Drug Chemistry backlog, which has been growing steadily since 2024. It should be noted that the Drug Chemistry unit completed 25% more requests the first 6 months of
2025 (1424) than in 2024 (1140), but submissions increased by 109% during that same time.
One last note, two recent Oregon Appellate Court rulings have also negatively impacted casework production. The Widerman opinion is resulting in more scientists spending more time in court and
therefore less time on the bench. For example, the DNA section spent twice the number of hours in court in 2025 as compared to the same time in 2024. The Adams ruling impacted the admissibility
of firearm comparison testimony. Managing that change has required significant resources from the Division and Firearms section. Although neither of these rulings impacted the KPM for work
performed in 2024, we anticipate that they will have a continuing impact for 2025 and beyond.

How Are We Doing
The Oregon State Police's ability to meet this performance measure depends on heavily staff resources within the Criminal Justice Information Services (CJIS) Division. Livescan technology and
automated processing improvements have significantly improved our ability to meet and/or exceed the set Manual Fingerprint Cards Average Turnaround time target of 4-days and the Electronic
Fingerprint Cards Average Turnaround time target of 2-hours.
For the most current calendar year (9/1/2024 - 8/31/2025, there were 114,682 total criminal cards received. There were 986 criminal arrest cards processed manually during the reporting period and
95% of the manual card total (938) were processed under the target turnaround time. Manual cards represented <1% of the total criminal arrest cards received. The manual card average turnaround
time for the reporting period was 1.38 days (33.20 hours).
For the most current calendar year (9/1/2024 - 8/31/2025) automated submissions, represented >99% of the total criminal arrest cards received. There were 113,696 cards received and processed
automatically. 112,946, or 99.8% of all automatic submissions were processed under the target turnaround time. The electronic card average turnaround time for the reporting period was 0.78 hours
(45.87 minutes).
Factors Affecting Results
Staffing levels, outliers, and agencies use of livescan technology have a direct effect on our performance results, as does the availability of our infrastructure systems. All of Oregon's county jail
facilities use livescan technology to submit their arrest fingerprint cards, with a growing number of local police departments also acquiring livescan technology. All agencies using livescan devices
submit fingerprints using the automated process. However, there continue to be instances where manually captured prints are necessary due to either an agency not having a livescan machine or
circumstances that make a manual print necessary (i.e., natural disaster, power issues, ADA compliance, etc.). Therefore, OSP will continue to maintain some level of accepted manual processing
services to meet this need. Outliers can include rejected cards or cards submitted incorrectly or need to gather additional information from the submission agency, as well as unexpected downtimes.
