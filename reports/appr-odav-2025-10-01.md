How Are We Doing
Pavement Maintenance Program (PMP) and the Pavement Evaluation Program (PEP) remain on track in 2025 and have been successful in completing the program work as scheduled.
To promote efficiency and encourage participation from DBE/COBID contractors, the program has divided its work into smaller regional contracts. This approach ensures smoother bidding processes
and allows for timely completion within the project’s schedule.
This KPM is measuring runways in fair or better condition as determined by the Pavement Condition Index (PCI).
Long-Term Impact
A ten-year study of the PMP, funded by the Federal Aviation Administration (FAA), revealed that the maintenance program extended the service life of airport pavement by over 20 years in typical
conditions. Considering the FAA's standard of 20 years for pavement longevity, this finding demonstrates that PMP efforts have effectively doubled the lifespan of airport pavements, potentially
avoiding an entire renovation cycle and yielding significant cost savings over time.
Factors Affecting Results
Several key factors can impact runway pavement conditions and program outcomes:
Weather and Fire Conditions
: Harsh or unpredictable weather or fire events can affect construction schedules and long-term pavement performance.
Availability of Matching Funds
: Securing of matching funds from either ODAV or the airport sponsor, which can delay or influence the scope of projects.
Construction Scheduling:
Coordination between contractors and project timelines is crucial for meeting program goals.
In the past, emphasis on crack sealing over surface treatments led to a decline in overall pavement condition. Recognizing this, the focus has now shifted to prioritizing surface sealants over smaller
crack repairs. This change has resulted in significantly improved pavement performance. A full PMP study was conducted, and ODAV has integrated key recommendations to enhance pavement
preservation efforts, ensuring long-term durability and efficiency.

How Are We Doing
FAA part 77.25 standards require a 20:1 glide slope for visual meteorological conditions (VMC) for public use airports. An airport does not meet this standard when obstructions (most commonly trees)
block the airport approach.  This KPM was changed during the 2025 session because it was previously measuring all public use airports including non-state airports and permanent obstructions.  Both
of these were not in ODAV's control.  Since the change the percentage has dramatically increased. ODAV has cleared or is in the process of clearing obstructions on 96% of state-owned airport
approaches.
Factors Affecting Results
The 28 state-owned airports each have 2 approaches and 8 of these have permanent obstructions.  Therefore, 48 airport approaches were measured.  Of these 46 have met the standard or have
obstruction clearing planned and are in the process of meeting the standard.  The two approaches that are not in the process of meeting the standard have trees that are not located on airport
property.

How Are We Doing
ODAV completed 141 out of 141 inspections or 100%.  This is the first time that ODAV has reached 100% on this standard. This KPM measures the number of airports that have received inspections
the past year in comparison with the total possible inspections. ODAV conducts 97 FAA sponsored inspections (5010s) over a three-year period. One third of the inspections are conducted annually
(29 in 2025). ODAV conducts quarterly self-inspections at 28 state owned airports. The total possible number of inspections is 141 this year.
Factors Affecting Results
In previous years the Owyhee Reservoir State Airport had several missed inspections due to it being inaccessible by land.  Recently ODAV has coordinated with the local sheriff's office to receive boat
rides to the airport to conduct the quarterly inspections.  ODAV airports are spread throughout the state and often in very remote areas.  Conducting airport inspections takes a great deal of planning
and coordination by ODAV's 4 airport operations staff.

How Are We Doing
ODAV continues to enhance its planning, monitoring, and closeout processes for federally funded airport projects. By maximizing the use of Non-Primary Entitlement (NPE) funds and Bipartisan
Infrastructure Law (BIL) funds, ODAV reduces the amount of unspent funds that would otherwise need to be returned to the Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) for discretionary allocation at other
airports. A key improvement is the recent transition to in-house procurement and contract administration, which has streamlined operations, resulting in faster turnaround times and improved
consistency in contract management.
Factors Affecting Results
ODAV owns and operates 28 public-use airports, with 12 of these being part of the National Plan of Integrated Airport Systems (NPIAS). Each non-primary NPIAS airport is eligible to receive up
to $150,000 annually in Airport Improvement Program (AIP) grant funds for qualifying projects. Additionally, BIL funds, which were enacted in 2021, must be obligated by 2026, influencing project
timelines and planning.

How Are We Doing

How Are We Doing
Over the past 3 years the results have been 95% or higher. The board and the agency have made efforts to improve on the areas that were shown to need improvement from past surveys.  To further
improve these results the agency and the board will need to work on the boards' involvement on key communications, aligning policy option packages with agency goals as well as reviewing board
best practices. The agency and the board recently completed a strategic plan and implementing this new plan will help to improve best practices for both the agency and the board. The agency and
the board will continue to strive to improve with a goal of meeting 100% of best board practices in the future.
Factors Affecting Results
Board members were asked to participate in an anonymous survey that asked fifteen yes or no questions addressing the Director and the Aviation Board Best Practices. These questions were based
on LFO and DAS best board practice recommendations that cover the board's involvement with the agency including key communications, agency goals, policy making, board training, finances,
budget as well as annual performance and review of the director. The Aviation Board is a 7-member board, and 5 members took the survey.
