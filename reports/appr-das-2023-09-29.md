How Are We Doing
OEA Note regarding the "Actual": This is submitting the September 2023 forecast General Fund figures for the 2023-25 BN, comparing them to the 2023 Close of Session forecast for the
2023-25 BN.
General Fund revenues overall in the 2023-25 biennium are coming in slightly above expectations. Based on the latest forecast, expectations are that Oregon’s unique kicker law will not be triggered
for the personal kicker, but will be triggered for the corporate kicker. Under Oregon’s kicker, errors larger than 2 percent are returned to taxpayers (for personal income taxes) as a credit on their tax
returns or retained in the General Fund for additional education spending (corporate income taxes). Based on the most recent forecast, the projected 2023-25 corporate kicker will be $321 million.
With two tax filing seasons still to come this biennium, there remains much uncertainty as to where state revenues will ultimately land.
Factors Affecting Results
The state’s volatile underlying economy and current tax structure yield larger year-to-year swings than in the typical state. While Oregon comes out ahead of the typical state in both an economic and
revenue sense over the entire business cycle, such variances are more challenging and difficult to accurately forecast.
Revenues in recent biennia have come in considerably higher than forecast and stronger than the underlying economy alone would suggest. This was in large part due to taxpayer’s response to the
large, federal tax reforms a few years ago, combined with federal aid during the pandemic, and record-setting asset markets in recent years. Additionally, the current inflationary economic boom has
actual target
also played a larger role in generating public revenues. Taxes are not collected on an inflation-adjusted basis, leading to rapid growth across all major tax instruments. The ongoing strength in
traditional corporate taxes this biennium is likely related to the continued impacts of the federal tax reform a few years ago.

How Are We Doing
The State of Oregon acknowledges racial equity must be at the forefront of our efforts and all other forms of diversity will benefit from these efforts. In addition, Department of Administrative Services
(DAS) is committed to advancing diversity, equity, inclusion and belonging (DEIB).
The current target is for the State and DAS to 100% reflect Oregon’s civilian workforce.
State of Oregon
As of the end of FY’23, the racial and ethnic diversity of the state workforce was 23.9%. This is an absolute increase of 0.8% from the previous fiscal year. While Oregon’s civilian workforce continues
to be more diverse than the Oregon State Government’s workforce, this key performance measure continues to show improvement.

Factors Affecting Results
State of Oregon
Diversity, equity, and inclusion (DEI) were a priority at the State of Oregon. In 2021, the Statewide
DEI Action Plan
was developed to resource State agencies to implement strategies toward
advancing DEI. This includes a specific strategy recommendation to “build a more diverse workforce and create an inclusive workforce” (strategy 8). Taking it a step further, Governor Kotek delivered
agency expectations which included a requirement that agencies have either a Diversity, Equity, and Inclusion Plan (DEI Plan) or a Plan for a Plan submitted by June 1, 2023. As a result, agencies
started developing DEI Plans by the end of FY’23, using the DEI Action Plan as a resource and guide. This process could have impacted hiring, promotions, and retention as agencies were
developing strategies to advance DEI and grow a more inclusive culture.
In addition to the expectation around DEI Plans, Governor Kotek’s expectations also included requirements related to hiring, which likely impacted hiring and promotions at the State:
Performance feedback for employees to be completed quarterly, starting on June 1, 2023.
Monitor hiring practices to ensure the average time to fill positions does not exceed more than 50 days.
New state employees were required to participate in a customer service training within 60 days of hire. This training was to be developed and implemented by June 1, 2023.
New employees were required to participate in Uplift Oregon’s benefits workshop within 30 days of hire support employees in making fully informed decisions when choosing benefits.
Managers new to State government were required to complete the introductory manager training program within 3 months of their new position, starting June 1, 2023.
Agencies also worked with the Office of Cultural Change to develop, integrate feedback, and implement Affirmative Action Plans for the upcoming 2023-2025 biennium “to improve hiring and
development opportunities.” The Affirmative Action Plans also continue “promoting Diversity, Equity, and Inclusion in the workplace, and the elimination of the effects of past and present
discrimination, intended or unintended” (Governor’s Executive Order 17-11).
While these activities were happening across the State enterprise, a DEI community of practice was also launched in partnership with Uplift Oregon. The Oregon Peer Equity Network (OPEN) was
launched in Winter 2022 and continues to meet regularly as a community of practice for State of Oregon staff on equity and justice in their agencies. The purpose of OPEN is to improve on and share
knowledge, as well as build relationships and common practices for DEI across the enterprise of State Government. This cohort has created opportunities for staff to not only engage around DEI, but
also resource and build relationships with one another to elevate this work. Learnings from this cohort will help inform how to approach a DEI community of practice moving forward.
The Office of Cultural Change, who leads DEI strategies at the State enterprise, underwent some transitions during FY’23. These transitions may have impacted or slowed progress toward advancing
DEI, even though this work was continuing during these shifts.
Department of Administrative Services (DAS)
While DEI and belonging are a priority at DAS, DAS is newer on its DEI journey. Specific to DAS, the transitions at the Office of Cultural Change includes vacancies and the office moving from the
Chief Human Resources Office (CHRO) to a new division, the Strategic Initiatives and Enterprise Accountability Division, and a Director of Cultural Change was hired at the end of FY’23. These
transitions, along with being newer on a DEI journey, may have impacted the racial/ethnic diversity of the department.
Even with these transitions, work was continuing in order to advance DEI. For an example of this work, the DAS CHRO started using Vidcruiter video interviewing as part of the hiring process. Video
interviewing can increase equity in hiring by:
Humanizing job applications by giving hiring managers opportunities to interview more candidates and in a shorter amount of time.
Allowing candidates to interview from home or another location that is accessible to them, which increases access to participate in interviews for people who face barriers due to lack of:
transportation, time to take off work to interview, childcare, etc.
Creating a more equitable evaluation process because candidates are asked the same interview questions in the same ways, within the same timeframes. Therefore, it minimizes unconscious
bias that may be happening in the delivering of the interview questions.
Lastly, DAS launched its strategic planning process at the end of FY’23. The strategic plan will integrate the department’s DEI Action Plan so that DEI is not a separate activity, but is integrated into
DAS’ values, priorities, and operations.

Factors Affecting Results
While significant (because the Salem area portion of the portfolio often makes up the costliest type of space in our portfolio), this data represents approximately 10% of the statewide transactions
during this measure period.
RES saw a large jump in the number of requested transactions statewide during this measure period.  Currently, RES is seeing trends of more movement in the Salem area, particularly consolidation
in state-owned buildings.
During the 2022-2023 measure period, RES also began to see a visible change in the real estate market conditions.  In comparison to the prior measure period, RES is finding a lot of available
inventory in the market, in major market areas.  This includes Portland, Salem/Keizer, Eugene, and Bend/Redmond.  However, outside of major market areas, inventory is still low, especially for small
or specialty requests.
Additionally, agencies have been moving towards making decisions to downsize.  Some agencies are simply no longer stagnating space-related decision making, while certain others have been more
proactive.  The proactive agencies have worked with RES to focus on consolidating into existing available space, utilizing state-owned space (which generally have slightly lower rates than market).
Furthermore, these agencies can otherwise plan to reduce or avoid certain costs when consolidating by looking at natural expirations first.  By terminating underutilized leased space based on
expiration dates, these agencies avoid payment of early termination fees and reduce relocation costs.
In combination, better inventory and increased agency movement have allowed RES to continue to negotiate below the average market rate.
However, RES has seen general inflationary pressures raising prices during this measure period.  RES is currently seeing trends indicating a downturn in the economy, such as the increase in
inventory in major market areas and continued high costs for construction and related materials.  Landlords are also continuing to cite inflation as a prime reason for seeking high initial and annual
inflation-related cost increases, as they try to negotiate with RES.

How Are We Doing
During the July 2022 – June 2023 reporting period, Enterprise Information Services, Cyber Security Services (EIS-CSS) transitioned from assessing Executive Branch agencies, boards, and
commissions against Version 7.1 of the Center for Internet Security (CIS) Critical Security Controls to Version 8.0 of the controls. This transition presented challenges in terms of updating and
developing the tools and processes required to assess the new set of controls, as well as the increased scope of controls being measured expanding from 41 to 65 (a 59% increase). As of this writing,
the Assessment Team has completed assessments on five Executive Branch agencies, boards and commissions, with another 12 assessments currently in progress.
EIS-CSS CIS Control Assessments measure agency, board, and commission implementation of the CIS Critical Security Controls and constituent safeguards according to at least two of the following
four criteria, depending on the Safeguard:
.
Whether or not procedures are established and documented to support the safeguard;
.
Whether or not the safeguard is implemented and, if so, to what degree (e.g. Not implemented, implemented on some systems, implemented on most systems, implemented on all
systems);
.
The degree to which safeguards are automated or technically enforced (If applicable); and
.
Whether or not the safeguard is regularly reviewed and reported to leadership (if applicable).
Each of the above criteria is evaluated and assigned a percentage score. For example, regarding Safeguard implementation, scoring options include:
actual target
Not Implemented (Defined as implemented on between 0% - 2% of in scope systems tested) = 0%
Implemented on Some Systems (Defined as implemented on between 3% - 50% of in scope systems tested) = 33%
Implemented on Most Systems (Defined as implemented on between 51% - 92% of in scope systems tested) = 66%
Implemented on All Systems (Defined as implemented on between 93% - 100% of in scope systems tested) = 100%
From the above assessment evaluation criteria, EIS leadership has chosen the second metric (whether or not the safeguard is implemented and, if so, to what degree) as the metric for Key
Performance Measure #8: Information Security. The average control implementation score across the five organizations assessed to date is 1.25 (implemented on some systems).
Factors Affecting Results
The most significant factor affecting agency implementation of the CIS Controls and constituent safeguards appears to be the existence and adoption of Enterprise solutions to meet the functional
requirements of the Safeguards. For example, the current assessment data shows that Executive Branch agencies, boards and commissions performed best in terms of network defense (CIS v8.0
Control 13) and vulnerability management (CIS v8.0 Control 7.) Conversely, agencies, boards, and commissions struggle in areas for which there are no Enterprise service offerings. Service provider
management (CIS v8.0 Control 15) and hardware asset inventory (CIS v8.0 Control 1) are two examples. For CIS controls that have no enterprise solution, assessors generally encounter a diverse
ecosystem of tools, systems, and processes ranging from spreadsheets to complex asset inventory and configuration management tools. Lacking a unified approach and standardized tool set
generally results in ad-hoc control implementation and insufficient control coverage and complicates top-down Enterprise guidance around the same.

How Are We Doing
In calendar year 2022, energy use in DAS buildings generated a calculated 17,035 metric tons of carbon dioxide equivalent GHG emissions (MTCO2e), or 0.0061300859 MTCO2e per square foot of
building space. Fuel use in the DAS owned and managed fleet generated 21,204 MTCO2e, or 0.00045358886 MTCO2e per vehicle mile traveled.
The state of Oregon’s GHG reduction goals for 2035 and 2050 reference 1990 as the baseline year. Since no agencies, including DAS, have building energy use or fleet records from 1990 sufficient to
calculate a GHG baseline for that year, DAS has been unable to calculate an actual percentage reduction compared to targets. In 2022, DAS developed a climate action planning tool that allows
agencies to set reduction targets with consideration of state GHG emission trends since 1990. DAS proposes to work with LFO/CFO to refine annual GHG targets for the next reporting cycle using the
tool, and factor in overall statewide GHG trends from 1990 to the current reporting year.
actual target
For both buildings and fleets, there are data reporting lags, with data for a prior year generally available by the middle of the following year. Future reporting years will include data from the prior
calendar year. This is due to the effort to collect, verify and process data across the fleet and building portfolio, and to calculate GHG emission factors for both fleet fuels consumed and building
energy provided by utilities. Data for both fleets and buildings are collected and reported by calendar year in accordance with DAS GHG data reporting requirements, as well as the Oregon
Department of Energy’s SEED reporting for state building energy use. Building energy use is reported for all DAS-owned buildings over 5,000 square feet in accordance with SEED reporting
requirements. This also applies to GHG emissions.
Factors Affecting Results
Covid and the resultant decrease in fleet vehicle use and building occupancy continued to have a significant impact during the 2021 reporting period due to building closures. Use of the DAS fleet by
agencies continued to be significantly lower during the reporting period from pre-Covid operations. While buildings were significantly less occupied, lighting and heating/cooling were still necessary to
continue operations in most buildings. DAS also continued operating with increased building ventilation to provide more fresh air to help reduce Covid impacts.
In DAS buildings, energy management efforts, including operational changes and capital projects, continued to drive down the energy use intensity (EUI) and resulting GHG emissions in many
buildings. In some cases, however, such as the need for extended operations to respond to Covid, wildfires and other issues, EUI increased during the reporting period. DAS also acquired the North
Valley Mall building in Wilsonville in 2020, which added both square footage and additional energy use to its portfolio.
