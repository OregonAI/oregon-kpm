How Are We Doing
On-water patrol has declined somewhat in recent years but is averaging around 24,620 hours over the last seven years.
NOTE: Number of Boat Patrol Hours in 2019 has been updated to reflect the correct amount.
Factors Affecting Results
The 2019-20 fiscal year budget reflected a reduction based on declining revenues and only six months of estimated revenues for the fee increase that took effect January 1, 2020. This amounted to a
3% reduction in budget that was reflected in available hours, but there are other factors at play as well.
The bottom line is affected by funding and buying power - enforcement funding has not kept up with inflation and labor costs resulting in fewer available hours to conduct marine patrol operations. A
long-term contributing factor is that many agencies used to provide matching funds. Due to declining O&C funds and other funding support for county enforcement agencies, those lost matching funds
contribute to the reduction in staffing and hours. In some situations, marine enforcement positions have gone unfilled due to a lack of viable candidates, which is part of a national trend. Another
contributing factor is the required additional training for law enforcement to maintain their certifications. This functionally means that "patrol hours" are converted to "program hours" and do not directly
support the marine patrol activities.

How Are We Doing
This year's BUII arrests were down markedly. The target of 100 appears unattainable for multiple reasons. Marine officers are still to be adequately trained to recognize and appropriately confront
impaired boaters, but reasons for the decline are numerous.
Factors Affecting Results
Multiple factors complicate law enforcements' capability to detect and process impaired operation. In the short term, a 2019 court case directly impacted how officers can detect impaired operation,
requiring extensive rewriting of laws related to DUII and BUII detection and prosecution. In the long term, enforcement officer turnover is high and increasing. BUII detection is a developed skill that
must be permanently practiced. Inexperienced officers struggle with applying classroom experience to on-water conditions. Lastly, some sheriffs and district attorney's lack the resources, capability or
desire to aggressively prosecute impaired operation, viewing boating as a recreational opportunity and discounting the serious risk of drug and alcohol impairment. This has a definite chilling effect on
officers' willingness to actively seek impaired operation.

How Are We Doing
This year has been very difficult, but not inconsistent given the high level of boating activity seen in Oregon and elsewhere in the United States. Most fatalities could easily have been prevented if
victims had been wearing a life jacket. Most were due to a very basic lack of understanding of dangers associated with boating. Also, the number skews high because eleven of the fatalities involved
non-motorized craft which are neither registered or for which operators are required to have any education course. If calculating the number of fatalities involving registered boats per 100,000
registered boats, we would be at 6.25 deaths per 100,000 registered boats, or slightly below target.
Factors Affecting Results
The COVID-19 pandemic is generally credited with encouraging people to look for simple and creative ways to social distance. Because schools and organized community activities were closed or
heavily restricted starting in March 2020, people looked for alternate activities. Eight fatalities occurred between March 21 and May 25 as people flocked to cold, swift waterways without good
preparation. Deputies reported historic levels of water-based recreation. Two fatalities involved boats striking non-boaters - a very concerning anomaly. Many involved falls overboard or simple paddle
craft capsizing.

How Are We Doing
Inspections completed across the state are 30% below, compared to 2019.
Factors Affecting Results
Since 2018, the state has been receiving additional federal funds via the USACOE to expand Oregon's operations beyond the state funded basic implementation level.  Consequently, this has allowed
Oregon Department of Fish and Wildlife (ODFW), our program partner, implementing the field operations to staff and operate additional boat inspection stations and to operate them for more months
during the year.  Two stations (Ashland and Ontario) are now operating year-round but with limited hours during the winter months with the Lakeview station not operating during 2020 due to no
submitted applications for the job when it was posted.
It appears that 2020 is trailing behind 2019 in the total number of inspections as this is most likely due to the COVID-19 pandemic. Stations opened a full month later than in past years and the state
pandemic shut-down executive order issued by the Governor significantly changed boaters plans to travel with their boats.  Boating traffic entering the state is where our inspections occur and so our
lower numbers could be a result of boaters deciding to stay within their home state and not travel as much.
