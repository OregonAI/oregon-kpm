Factors Affecting Results
DAS updated its methodology to ensure that feedback represents the views of the agency’s most direct customers—those individuals who most frequently interact with the agency and its services.
Historically, DAS’ methodology for reporting on this KPM was to distribute a one-time survey to a random sampling of State employees requesting feedback on: (1) whether they have interacted with
DAS in the last calendar year (2) the services they interacted with (3) the quality of those services. This methodology resulted in many responses from employees with limited direct interaction with
DAS, which likely influenced 2022 results. In order to have results that are more specific and actionable to its various services, DAS needed to better identify and engage its customers.
DAS updated its methodology for compiling DAS Customer Service feedback to reflect 3 key goals:
(1) Survey individuals with a record of at least some interaction with a DAS service area
(2) Survey customers within 30-60 days of interaction for more specific and accurate feedback
(3) Empower DAS Program areas to have a closer connection to our customers by giving them the ability to implement changes/improvements based on customer feedback.
DAS currently has 14 service program areas with their own Customer Satisfaction survey which is distributed to their respective customers every quarter. This data can now be collected more
consistently, more accurately, and aggregated over time to better reflect the overall Satisfaction of DAS Services.

OEA Note regarding the "Actual": This is submitting the September 2024 forecast General Fund figures for the 2023-25 BN, comparing them to the 2023 Close of Session forecast for the
2023-25 BN.
General Fund revenues overall in the 2023-25 biennium are coming in above expectations. Based on the latest forecast, expectations are that Oregon’s unique kicker law will be triggered for both the
personal kicker and for the corporate kicker. Under Oregon’s kicker, errors larger than 2 percent are returned to taxpayers (for personal income taxes) as a credit on their tax returns or retained in the
General Fund for additional education spending (corporate income taxes). Based on the most recent forecast, the projected 2023-25 personal kicker is $987 million, while the corporate kicker will be
$883 million. With one tax filing season still to come this biennium, there remains much uncertainty as to where state revenues will ultimately land.
Factors Affecting Results
The state’s volatile underlying economy and current tax structure yield larger year-to-year swings than in the typical state. While Oregon comes out ahead of the typical state in both an economic and
revenue sense over the entire business cycle, such variances are more challenging and difficult to accurately forecast.
Revenues in recent biennia have come in considerably higher than forecast and stronger than the underlying economy alone would suggest. This was in large part due to taxpayer’s response to the
large, federal tax reforms a few years ago, combined with federal aid during the pandemic, and record-setting asset markets in recent years. Additionally, the current inflationary economic boom has
actual target also played a larger role in generating public revenues. Taxes are not collected on an inflation-adjusted basis, leading to rapid growth across all major tax instruments. The ongoing
strength in traditional corporate taxes this biennium is likely related to the continued impacts of the federal tax reform a few years ago and record-high corporate profits nationally.

The State of Oregon acknowledges racial equity must be at the forefront of our efforts and all other forms of diversity will benefit from these efforts. In addition, Department of Administrative Services
(DAS) is committed to advancing diversity, equity, inclusion and belonging (DEIB).
The current target for the State of Oregon and the Department of Administrative Services Workforce Diversity measure is to be 100% reflective of Oregon’s civilian workforce.

to be more diverse than the Oregon State Government’s workforce, this key performance measure continues to show improvement.
Calculation: 24.9% / 30.0% = 83% of the goal of 100% reflection of the Oregon workforce.  (This calculation is based on the states State racial/ethnic diversity percent of 24.9% divided by Oregon

Factors Affecting Results
State of Oregon
All Executive Branch State agencies submitted a Diversity, Equity and Inclusion Plan (DEI Plan) by June 1, 2024, which was an expectation set by Governor Kotek. Not only did agencies submit a DEI
Plan, but they met with the Office of Cultural Change to discuss feedback on their plan, and participate in quarterly cohorts with other agencies for support in capacity building and implementing their
plans.
In addition to the expectation around DEI Plans, Governor Kotek’s expectations also included requirements related to hiring, which likely impacted hiring and promotions at the State:
Performance feedback for employees to be completed quarterly.
Monitor hiring practices to ensure the average time to fill positions does not exceed more than 50 days.
New state employees were required to participate in a customer service training within 60 days of hire.
New employees were required to participate in Uplift Oregon’s benefits workshop within 30 days of hire support employees in making fully informed decisions when choosing benefits.
Managers new to State government were required to complete the introductory manager training program within 3 months of their new position.
Agencies also worked with the Office of Cultural Change to develop, integrate feedback, and implement Affirmative Action Plans “to improve hiring and development opportunities.” The Affirmative
Action Plans also continue “promoting Diversity, Equity, and Inclusion in the workplace, and the elimination of the effects of past and present discrimination, intended or unintended” (Governor’s
Executive Order 17-11). Agency Affirmative Action plans have been submitted to the Office of Cultural Change in August 2024 and are currently under review.
One shift since the last report, the Oregon Peer Equity Network (OPEN), an enterprise DEI community of practice in partnership with Uplift Oregon, completed its 1-year cohort. They continued to meet
quarterly throughout FY’23-24 to continue momentum and engagement, but the OPEN program was recently cut from Uplift’s program offerings. As a result, the Office of Cultural Change is in
conversation with OPEN cohort members to explore supports and next steps in potential engagement. The effects of this decision could impact the next KPM report.
The Office of Cultural Change fully staffed its office during FY’23-24, which is the first year the office has been fully staffed since it was formed. With this change comes with additional DAS and
enterprise-wide supports for DEI that the Office of Cultural Change is currently building.

The Office of Cultural Change also enhanced partnerships with multiple divisions across DAS, building relationships, engagement, and capacity building with divisions to grow equitable practices

How Are We Doing
Similar to the prior measure period, DAS Real Estate Services (RES) observed stagnant vacancy rates across the office markets, with increasing expenses.
Inflation during the measure period was well above economist targets, and while current economic growth can be partly attributed to high employment numbers, Salem and Portland central business
district (CBD) continue to have a high office vacancy rate due to the effects of the pandemic on the workforce, and there are still challenges as workers begin to return to the office (and with what
frequency).
Inflationary pressures such as increased construction costs and higher interest rates, additional service costs (such as additional janitorial due to the COVID-19 pandemic), drastic increases in utility
costs, and increases in insurance rates have resulted in increased asking rental rates in the commercial real estate market along with substantial annual rental escalators.
Agency decision-making relating to office space was stagnant last measure period. During the current measure period agencies, after receiving increased direction from DAS leadership in late 2023,
began to move and make decisions relating to office space. This increased direction, decision-making, and movement, along with higher office vacancy in major market areas such as Salem and
Portland, have allowed RES to negotiate office rates well below market rates and allow for increased concessions, for this measure period.
With additional pressure added from increased vacancy rates, RES has been able to negotiate low rental rates (below market) with increased concessions, while embarking on rightsizing the privately
leased portfolio, by consolidating smaller offices, negotiating differing lease rent structures (which help both agencies and landlords separately track and account for lease expenses), and negotiating
added value tenant improvements or rent concessions to offset inflationary annual increases. RES is also seeing the result of rental rates below market, due to the exercise of previously negotiated
actual target
extension options. In addition, executing longer term leases, due to prior cost-saving initiatives, have also allowed RES to negotiate rental rates below market rates. Because a large portion of the
portfolio has now benefited from negotiations of longer terms, lower annual escalators and many Landlord-provided tenant improvements this means that, in future measure periods RES expects to
see a lower likelihood of dramatic savings.

Factors Affecting Results
To arrive at the results, RES considers the market rate for each office space transaction (which is based on the dynamic factors of each lease, including the condition and age of the office building, its
location, the neighborhood environment, services and amenities offered and provided by the Landlord, any improvements, or other pertinent considerations such as building sustainability or increased
utility and service costs) in the Salem market. During this measure period, the Salem market made up a smaller percentage of the transactions statewide as in the last measure period. Going forward,
RES will make the same determination as compared to a market rate analysis (such as from COSTAR) for the same market, type, and class of building.
During the 2023-2024 measure period, RES also began to see changes in real estate market conditions. RES is currently seeing both increases in vacancy, which results in an augmentation of
inventory in major market areas and continued high costs for construction and related materials (in part due to increased interest rates). Landlords are also continuing to cite interest rates and inflation
as a prime reason for seeking high initial and annual inflation-related cost increases, as they try to negotiate with RES. RES has also seen trends of increased utility and service costs in private sector
office leases. In comparison to the prior measure period, RES is finding a marked increase in inventory in major markets, including Salem and Portland, particularly when compared to more outlying
areas. Inventory remains low outside major market areas, making it difficult for RES to locate spaces for small square footage or for specialty requests, such as lab space.
As RES is encouraging analysis of lease and space utilization, proactive agencies have begun to plan lease administration strategy to reduce or avoid certain lease costs. Agencies are:
electing not to renew leases,
negotiating early terminations of unneeded spaces,
reconfiguring existing locations,
looking for space-saving consolidation opportunities, particularly in existing long-term leases or state-owned properties.
Increased agency movement, combined with early planning and decision-making, are major factors in allowing RES to continue to negotiate below the average market rental rate across the state.
As RES continues to assist agencies with analysis of lease and space utilization for of future office space needs, RES anticipates that many agencies will change or reduce their office space due to
the new hybrid work modality and to conform with the new space requirements and policies. When RES negotiates a long-term lease that is expiring, which is locked in at a lower rental rate, RES
anticipates that the new lease negotiation (whether it be a renewal or a new location), will “reset” to the current (likely higher) market rate. In other words, although state tenants may experience a cost
savings from consolidating locations (or otherwise reducing square footage), RES expects the newly negotiated rental rate (and cost per square foot) to be more reflective of the higher market rate (or
much closer thereto).

How Are We Doing
It is difficult to measure OregonBuys adoption utilizing the three metrics described above. This is further explained below in the additional sections.
Metric 1 - SPOTS Card spend
- OregonBuys is not suited for capturing SPOTS Card transactions. DAS State Procurement Service’s (SPS) eProcurement (ePro) Team actually instructs state agency
personnel not to use OregonBuys to capture SPOTS card transactions as the system does not originate those transactions and the transactions are not carried out within the system. Due to stringent
Treasury rules surrounding PCI compliance, the system is not allowed to carry out these transactions.
Due to Department of Treasury rules related to PCI compliance, OregonBuys cannot capture the necessary SPOTS card spend. Downloading SPOTS Card transactions data into OregonBuys is not
encouraged as advised by SPS ePro Team. This measure is not a good measure of OregonBuys adoption as SPOTs card purchases are independent of OregonBuys and due to rules, these
transactions are not completing within the system.

How Are We Doing
In calendar year 2023, energy use in DAS buildings generated a calculated10,580 metric tons of carbon dioxide equivalent GHG emissions (MTCO2e), or 0.003807 MTCO2e per square foot of
building space. Fuel use in the DAS owned and managed fleet generated 22,314 MTCO2e, or 0.000647 MTCO2e per vehicle mile traveled.
The state of Oregon’s GHG reduction goals for 2035 and 2050 reference 1990 as the baseline year. Since no agencies, including DAS, have building energy use or fleet records from 1990 sufficient to
calculate a GHG baseline for that year, DAS has been unable to precisely calculate an actual percentage reduction compared to targets. For this reporting year, DAS used a methodology provided in a
climate action planning tool developed for agencies in 2022 to estimate emission increases or decreases relative to the 1990 baseline. These estimations use statewide GHG emissions trend data for
the buildings and transportation sectors from 1990 onward as a proxy for GHG emissions trends in DAS operations.
actual target
For both buildings and fleets, data for a prior year is generally available by the middle of the following year. This is due to the effort to collect, verify and process data across the fleet and building
portfolio.  Data for both fleets and buildings are collected and reported by calendar year in accordance with DAS GHG data reporting requirements, as well as the Oregon Department of Energy’s
SEED reporting for state building energy use. Building energy use is reported for all DAS-owned buildings over 5,000 square feet in accordance with SEED reporting requirements. This also applies to
GHG emissions.
Factors Affecting Results
Hybrid work and low building occupancy continue to affect building and fleet GHG emissions. While buildings continued to be significantly less occupied in 2023 relative to pre-Covid levels, lighting
and heating/cooling were necessary to continue operations. DAS continues to invest in energy management efforts, including operational changes and capital projects. Natural gas use continues to be
a significant source of building GHGs. DAS also acquired the North Valley Mall building in Wilsonville in 2020, which added square footage and additional energy use to its portfolio.
While DAS continues to add electric, hybrid, and plug-in hybrid vehicles to its state fleet, they are not yet sufficient in number to decrease fleet-related GHG emissions. As more of these vehicles are
added in accordance with state statute, GHG emissions should start to decrease.
In 2023, DAS purchased 12,948,000 kilowatt hours of renewable energy credits (RECs) from Portland General Electric to offset its energy use and GHG emissions. As a result, reported DAS building
GHG emissions in 2023 were significantly lower relative to prior years. While not seen as a sole method to reduce GHG emissions, DAS will continue to purchase these RECs while also investing
resources to reduce energy use and GHGs in its building portfolio.
