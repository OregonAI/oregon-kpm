How Are We Doing
Every two years the Governor's office conducts a survey to assess its performance in meeting the customer satisfaction measure for Regional Solutions (RS). The survey assesses services provided by Regional Solutions Coordinators and state agency team members. The most recent Regional Solutions survey was
conducted during July and August of 2024.
The survey was completed by 260 respondents (compared to 188 in 2018, 250 in 2020, and 222 in 2022). Most respondents were the primary customers of Regional Solutions such as local elected leaders, local government staff, and local/regional economic development practitioners. Other respondents included state
and federal staff and private sector representatives. Responses were submitted from around the state, with at least ten responses from each of the eleven regions.
The Regional Solutions KPM target is set at 90% to serve as a motivator for continually improving state agency service delivery to local jurisdictions and businesses. The survey measures how close Regional Solutions is to that 90% target for overall customer service and five specific areas of service (Timeliness,
Accuracy, Helpfulness, Expertise, and Availability of Information), per the DAS Guidelines for customer satisfaction surveys.
The 2024 survey revealed that Regional Solutions continues to provide exceptional customer service, as evidenced by the high percentage of respondents rating Regional Solutions service as "Excellent to Good". The key indicators all received high marks, with some room for growth in the Accuracy category, likely due to
the learning curve experienced by new staff. As the team of Regional Solutions Coordinators continues to deepen their expertise in their positions, we expect the program’s ability to provide accurate information the first time will show improvement. Fewer than 2% of respondents scored a Poor rating in all categories.
We also heard from several respondents that there is opportunity to increase familiarity with the program, showing that Regional Solutions has a great chance to strengthen newly built relationships and educate partners on the many ways the program can be of service. In the year since the survey, RS staff have been
working with community leaders and other partners to deepen connections.
When asked about the strengths of Regional Solutions and how the program has added value to their organization or community, respondents were particularly appreciative of RS work in bringing together stakeholders on complex projects and initiatives, including housing and infrastructure improvements, and increasing
government efficiency by helping local communities navigate state programs and processes. Other common responses highlighted the program’s ability to convene/collaborate, build relationships, provide a direct connection to the Governor’s Office, share information, and solve problems. Comments included:

This has been valuable for multiple projects that were facing issues that would otherwise been more difficult and time consuming to resolve.”

And importantly, if they cannot help, they direct you to other resources. Knowing that I can rely on a RS ‘team’ to get answers to
questions quickly has saved countless amounts of money and time.”

Factors Affecting Results
Following high turnover in the Regional Solutions staff due to the gubernatorial transition, Regional Solutions hired several new Regional Coordinators who started at the beginning of the 23-25 biennium. With similarly high turnover in local elected leaders and partners Regional Solutions engages with, this new staff team
made a concerted effort to grow the program’s reach and connect with new community partners during their first year in their positions. The team conducted extensive outreach to introduce themselves to communities and offer support, nearly tripling the list of partners served by the program. While this increased the reach
of the program, it also means that many stakeholders are still learning about the services Regional Solutions can provide.
The Regional Solutions program continues to face a high demand for its services compared to its limited resources. The teams must constantly balance the need to work with communities to move high priority projects forward, while trying to be available for all communities that request assistance. In addition, they must be
flexible to respond to the widely varying capacity of partners to work on economic and community development, i.e., depending on jurisdiction size, resources, and related factors.

Coverage:
Procurement data in this report was restricted to those entered into the states’ central eProcurement system (OregonBuys) only.  It does not include procurements made via the State Purchasing-Card of Oregon Transaction System (SPOTS) or other off-system mechanisms.  SPOTS purchases represent a substantial
share of small-dollar, day-to-day agency spending and off-system data cannot be consistently linked back to contract records.  Therefore, the percentages reported here represent only a portion of state procurement activity and do not reflect the full scope of statewide spending.
Additionally reporting practices and system use varied across state agencies.  OregonBuys replaced ORPIN in July 2021, and most agencies implemented Phase II procure-to-pay functionality by September 2023, but several agencies have yet to fully adopt Phase II functionality (requisitions, purchases orders, receiving,
invoicing) as of FY25.  The following executive agencies had some degree of exemption from full OregonBuys utilization at least through FY25:
Oregon Department of Transportation
Oregon Department of Fish and Wildlife

Summary
In FY25, COBID-certified firms accounted for 2.5% of total dollars awarded and 6.2% of procurements recorded in OregonBuys. This is a larger share of procurements than in years prior, but it remains primarily constituted of small dollar value contracts. FY25 figures reveal opportunities that help firms build capacity and
experience, yet they do not substantially shift overall spend toward the statewide target of 11%.  Additionally, a large share of COBID participation occurs through subcontracting and the state does not yet have a reliable method to track subcontractors, likely understating the true level of COBID participation. System gaps,
agency exemptions, and inconsistent reporting practices prevent a complete view of statewide procurement activity, yet the performance measure remains a reasonable proxy for understanding COBID participation under present conditions.

Management Comments:
Executive Order and Disparity Study
Executive Order 22-
15 and the work stemming from the statewide Disparity Study continue to guide Oregon’s equity in public contracting efforts. In August 2025, DAS released the updated Equity in Procurement Dashboard, developed in close partnership with COBID that provides all data monthly, which integrates certification data with OregonBuys procurement activity. This tool provides
agencies with a clear picture of how they are performing against the 11% statewide benchmark for directing goods and services contract dollars to COBID-certified firms. Business Oregon is proud to be among the agencies already meeting this target, and COBID
remains committed to helping partners statewide reach and exceed this goal through data sharing, training, and technical assistance.
At the same time, Oregon is preparing for another transformative step forward with the passage of House Bill 2337, which directs DAS to create new Small Business Procurement Program. COBID will administer the Oregon Small Business Enterprise (OSBE) certification, designed to open access to public contracting for
more Oregon’s small businesses across the state. By aligning this effort with the Executive Order and Disparity Study recommendations, COBID is ensuring that policy, practice, and engagement strategies work together to advance equity, transparency, and opportunity in public procurement.
COBID Operations
The past year has been one of historic progress for COBID, with nearly 700 new firms added since last year’s report, the largest increase in our program’s history. This growth reflects the success of expanded outreach, streamlined processes, and clearer communication with Oregon businesses.
Operational improvements have transformed the certification experience. Average processing times for new applications were reduced to under 42 days and renewals to less than two days. COBID also simplified requirements by eliminating the three-year
recertification cycle and notary submissions, while implementing annual CPI-based reviews of ESB thresholds and new tools such as certification badges to help firms market their certification status.
Outreach and engagement expanded to every region of the state, with COBID participating in more than 55 events from Ontario to Astoria and reaching nearly 5,000 participants. These efforts, supported by dedicated outreach staff and improved reporting tools, have strengthened partnerships with agencies,
municipalities, and community organizations.
This year also marked the highest customer satisfaction results in COBID’s history, rising to 93%. To build on this momentum, COBID just launched the Statewide COBID Advisory Group, bringing together certified business owners, agency partners, and community
stakeholders from across Oregon. The group will provide direct input on access to opportunities, application processes and program improvements, ensuring decisions are shaped by the lived experience of small businesses. Meeting monthly in its first year and then
quarterly, the Advisory Group will serve as a permanent forum for collaboration, accountability, and continuous improvement in Oregon’s equity in contracting work.
COBID Staffing
COBID is now fully staffed with 12 team members, providing the capacity needed to meet the needs of Oregon’s small businesses in a timely and effective manner. The team includes six Certification Specialist 2 positions dedicated to processing applications, two
Compliance Specialist 3 positions responsible for complex reviews and policy alignment, and two Program Analysts focused on outreach, engagement, reporting, and continuous improvement. These staff are supported by administrative personnel who ensure businesses receive clear guidance, responsive service, and
accessible entry points into certification.
With this structure in place, COBID has built the stability and expertise to sustain faster processing times, strengthen compliance, and expand engagement across the state. Being fully staffed marks a turning point for the program—ensuring that small businesses not only gain certification efficiently but also receive the
support they need to connect with opportunities and grow.
Business Equity Manager Remarks:
In FY2025, COBID utilization increased to 2.51% of awarded dollars, up from 1.09% in FY2024. This is the first time in a decade that results have moved past the 2% mark, showing real progress. While total statewide spend recorded in OregonBuys declined sharply this year ($2.18B vs. $4.31B in FY2024), COBID awards
grew in both number (422 contracts, up from 370) and value ($54.7M, up from $47.0M). This indicates the improvement reflects an actual shift in share, not just broader spending patterns.
Even with this progress, the results reinforce the same underlying issue, COBID firms remain underutilized. COBID’s growth in certifications, outreach, and operational capacity has strengthened the pipeline of available firms. System gaps, limited subcontractor tracking, exemptions from OregonBuys, and inconsistent
reporting continue to impact reporting accuracies.
The increase this year is a big step forward, but the broader problem remains. Until agencies consistently use the tools available and are held accountable for results, COBID utilization will continue to fall short of the capacity and contribution that Oregon’s small and diverse businesses are ready to deliver.

How Are We Doing
The annual targets for workforce diversity combine the representation of people of color and people with a reported disability in the civilian workforce. This annual target is 22% of State employees, which currently includes 46,882 people. In FY’24-25, 27% of the State of Oregon’s workforce was comprised of people of
color and people with a reported disability. While this exceeds the target, it is a decrease from the previous year, which was 30%. The 30% figure from the last fiscal year seems to be an outlier, as it was quite a large increase compared to all the previous years. If reviewing the data assuming that last year was an outlier,
the proportion of people of color and people with a reported disability is continuing to trend upwards.
The percentage of females in the workforce continues to stay at 56% for 3 years in a row. Regarding people with a reported disability, this percentage decreased from 6% to 2%. The percentage of people of color in the State workforce continues to increase for the past 5 years, with 26% of the workforce identifying as
people of color, which is an increase of 1%.
While the total combined percentage of people of color and people with a reported disability had a notably high rate of increase least year, followed by a decrease this year, the proportion of people of color increased. The biggest decrease seems to come from the percentage of people with a reported disability. It is unclear
what caused the big increase in the previous fiscal year, and what is causing the large decrease back down to similar numbers from previous years.
When looking at the disaggregated data for new hires, promotions, and resignations:
Females represent 56% of the State workforce, and the proportion of new hires and promotions who are female is higher than their representation in the workforce (58% and 61%, respectively). Females represent just over half of resignations (51%). Females represent a higher proportion of new hires during FY’24-
25 (58%) compared to the previous fiscal year (55%).
People of color represent 27% of the State workforce, and the proportion of new hires was much higher than their representation in the workforce (35%). While FY’23-24 showed that 40% of people of color received promotions, FY’24-25 numbers are lower at 27%, which matches the percentage of the workforce but
demonstrates a decrease in promotions between this year and last year. Among resignations, 26% were people of color, which is slightly lower than their representation in the workforce and lower than the previous fiscal year, which was 28%.
People with a reported disability represent 2% of the workforce. While promotion rates seem to match the workforce numbers at 2%, new hires were lower (1%), and resignations were higher (3%). This may have contributed to the large decrease of people with a reported disability working at the State from the
previous year. Note that in the previous report, people with a reported disability represent 6% of the workforce, but were hired, promoted, and resigned at lower rates than their representation in the workforce (1%, 2%, and 3%, respectively). This also may have contributed to a downward trend that is present in this
fiscal year.
Some other data to highlight comparing the State workforce to the labor force demographics in Oregon:
In Oregon’s labor force, females represent 43%. The State’s workforce has a higher percentage of females at 56%.
When disaggregated, the State of Oregon’s workforce has a smaller percentage of people with a reported disability (2%) and people of color (27%) than Oregon’s labor force as a whole (8% and 30%, respectively).
Factors Affecting Results
A large factor impacting Oregon and the nation during this time is a divestment from diversity, equity and inclusion (DEI) initiatives in general due to changing federal priorities and political climate at the national level, which have been impacting funding of DEI programs at the local level. This was also reported during the
previous fiscal year’s KPM report, and impacts have likely increased this year as divestments from DEI continue to grow. While the State of Oregon has not shifted from its DEI commitments and values, the workforce has likely been impacted by this divestment, and it is possible that this will continue to be reflected in
future data.
This fiscal year also marks the second full year of Governor Kotek’s agency expectations related to hiring, promotions, and retention. These expectations include:
Performance feedback for employees to be completed quarterly.
Monitor hiring practices to ensure the average time to fill positions does not exceed more than 50 days.
New state employees were required to participate in a customer service training within 60 days of hire.
New employees were required to participate in Uplift Oregon’s benefits workshop within 30 days of hire support employees in making fully informed decisions when choosing benefits.
Managers new to State government were required to complete the introductory manager training program within 3 months of their new position.
In addition, Governor Kotek’s expectations required agencies to have either a Diversity, Equity, and Inclusion Plan completed no later than June 1, 2024. As a result, nearly every single agency in the Executive Branch has a DEI Plan in place that they are actively implementing. The Office of Cultural Change (OCC)
supported each agency in their DEI Plan development by meeting with agencies to give feedback on their plans and have set up quarterly cohort meetings to provide ongoing support for their DEI Plan implementation. The OCC has also implemented an annual progress report check-in with agencies to support, resource,
and ensure that implementation outlined in agency DEI Plans are underway.
Agencies also worked with the OCC to develop, integrate feedback, and implement Affirmative Action Plans “to improve hiring and development opportunities.” The Affirmative Action Plans also continue “promoting Diversity, Equity, and Inclusion in the workplace, and the elimination of the effects of past and present
discrimination, intended or unintended” (Governor’s Executive Order 17-11). Agencies are required to share progress on affirmative action in their updated plans, which were due and submitted with Agency Request Budgets.
This fiscal year also expanded the Racial Equity Impact Statement (REIS) process to include all Executive Branch agencies so that they can each consider racial impacts in their budget proposals. Previous years required that a select number of agencies submit an REIS with their Agency Request Budget. With all
actual target
Executive Branch agencies participating in the REIS process, this could have potentially impacted budget decisions regarding new hires and promotions.

All of the datasets (State of Oregon and American Community Survey) report gender as a binary with “male” and “female” categories.
