How Are We Doing
The previous metric for this Key Performance Metric (KPM) was to reduce fatal crashes on state and interstate highways where OSP has primary responsibility by 2.5% annually (from 2012 to 2022),
reaching a target of 101 fatal crashes by 2022.  Due to factors beyond OSP’s control, such as licensed number of drivers and vehicle miles traveled, it was unlikely OSP would be able to achieve a
purely nominal target.
Instead, OSP changed the metric based on the number of fatal crashes vehicle miles traveled (VMT) on highways segments where Oregon State Police has primary responsibility.  In 2015, the fatal
crash rate per 1,000,000,000 VMT was 7.79.  Between 2010 - 2015, the lowest fatal crash rate per 1,000,000,000 VMT was 5.63 (in 2013).  Using an average of 100 fatal crashes per 1,000,000,000
VMT over a six-year period (2010 - 2015), OSP’s new target is a fatal crash rate of 5.06 or lower per 1,000,000,000 VMT.
In calendar year 2019 (the most recent validated data from ODOT’s Crash Analysis Unit), the fatal crash rate per 1,000,000,000 VMT was 8.60.  This is lower than the 9.19 fatal crash rate in calendar
year 2018, but still higher than 6.74 average fatal crash rate between 2010 – 2015.
Although the fatal crash rate is higher than the target, it should be noted that the rate in 2019 was down 6.4% compared to 2018 and reflects the lowest rate since 2015.
Factors Affecting Results
Enforcement plans focused on changing driving behaviors in an effort to reduce crashes are developed and implemented.  Patrol offices around the state also work with ODOT and other partners on
engineering and education efforts aimed at reducing crashes.  There are other variables that affect crash rates outside the control of OSP’s enforcement, education, engineering and emergency
medical services efforts.  Some of these variables include the economy, adverse weather events, number of licensed drivers, and changes in annual vehicle miles traveled.  In fact, the number of
vehicle miles travelled on Oregon’s highways in calendar year 2019 was the highest on record (21,861,800,000).  It is worth noting VMT has a positive correlation to the number of fatal crashes on
state-owned highways, which helps explain the recent elevation in the crash rate (calendar year 2016 forward) compared to the previous average rate of 6.74 (2010 – 2015).

How Are We Doing
The Oregon State Police (OSP) assumed the Ignition Interlock Device (IID) program formally from the Oregon Department of Transportation in July 2019, which means this report reflects the first full
year of compliance data collected by OSP.  The most recent available data from the Oregon Driver and Motor Vehicles Division (June 2021) indicate there were 30,401 drivers with an IID
Requirement.  Conversely, the most recent IID installation data shows there were 6,843 devices installed (August 2021), which translates to a compliance rate of 22.5%.  While we did not reach our
target of a 45% compliance rate, the 23% compliance rate is an improvement over last year’s result of 19%.  This is due to installation numbers increasing steadily from 6,424 in 2020 to 6,843 as of
August 2021.
Factors Affecting Results
During 2020, COVID-19 restrictions impacted the ability of OSP members to proactively enforce the requirements of the IID program.  Likewise, COVID-19 restrictions also had negative impacts on
the ability for some participants to meet their mandated requirements.  As a result, OSP implemented temporary administrative rules, thus taking into account considerations where people were unable
to be fully compliant within the program.  This had an impact of the count of the number of required IID devices being installed.  The Department is continuing to conduct service center inspections and
will continue working to increase education efforts.

How Are We Doing
In calendar year 2020, the Fish and Wildlife Division achieved 76,647 angler and hunter contacts, achieving approximately 90% percent of the target level of 85,000 angler and hunter contacts per
year.  This results in an approximate 9% decrease in contacts when compared to 2019 and is the fewest contacts made since 2017.
Factors Affecting Results
The KPM target was nearly reached in 2019 and 2018, achieving a 99% and 98% contact rate respectively.  However, 2020 was an unprecedented year with the COVID-19 pandemic, which certainly
impacted the Division’s ability to achieve the target.  As was mentioned in last year’s report, the Division had the most vacancies in recent history due to funding shortfalls related to COVID-19.  The
closure of recreational fishing, hunting, crabbing and clamming to anyone who resided outside of Oregon due to COVID-19 had an impact on the number of contacts the Fish and Wildlife Division
made.  In addition, an unprecedented wildfire season and civil unrest pulled many Fish and Wildlife Troopers away from their primary assignments, thus impacting the number of contacts made.

In calendar year 2020, Fish and Wildlife Division Troopers detected 1,315 illegally harvested fish and wildlife species, missing the target by approximately 34%.  This is the most percentage points the

harvested fish and wildlife species.

How Are We Doing
The number of Drug Trafficking Organizations (DTOs) the Oregon State Police (OSP) assisted in dismantling or disrupting increased from 22 in 2013 (reported in 2014), to 30 in 2014 and 2015
(reported in 2015 and 2016 respectively).  For calendar year 2016, that number jumped to 59 and in 2017, the number of DTOs dismantled or disrupted was 52.  In calendar year 2018, OSP met the
target by dismantling or disrupting 25 DTOs.  It is worth noting the target for the number of DTOs dismantled or disrupted was increased from 20 to 25 beginning in 2018.  In calendar year 2019, the
target was increased again from 25 to 35 which was surpassed significantly when 73 DTOs were dismantled or disrupted.  However, in 2020, the number of dismantled or disrupted DTOs dropped to
29, missing the target by 17%.
Factors Affecting Results
Task forces vary in their mission and focus to target street-level, mid-level or upper-level drug investigations.  Mid to upper-level narcotic investigations, focusing on DTO’s, are more complex and
longer in duration while street level investigations tend to be direct in nature and short-term.  The increase in DTO’s dismantled or disrupted can be attributed to the on-going, widespread, consistent
and reinforced knowledge of the definition of a DTO that has been standardized by the Office of National Drug Control Policy.  A concerted effort has been made by drug task forces to accurately
capture data based on the standardized DTO criteria and submit documentation to HIDTA and/or the appropriate law enforcement reporting agency reflecting their efforts and end results.  In addition,
OSP has had a consistent, tenured presence on drug task forces statewide, particularly those larger in size, that have a greater case volume and are focused on higher level illegal drug activity.
The Drug Enforcement Section (DES) of OSP experienced many challenges during the 2020 calendar year, including a reduction in drug cases.  Challenges leading to the decrease in drug seizures
include travel and training restrictions due to COVID-19, lower levels of traffic enforcement due to a decrease of traffic volume, and more restrictive case law rulings.  An unprecedented wildfire season
and civil unrest pulled many detectives away from their primary assignments, which included drug enforcement.  Additionally, budget uncertainties due Ballot Measure 110 and COVID-19 impacts
resulted in 10 DES Detective positions being held vacant for most of the year.

How Are We Doing
In the 2020 calendar year, the percentage of analytical requests completed in 30 days or less was 22%, which was a significant step down from 2019 (28%).  Additionally, the Forensic Division saw a
4% increase in the number of requests that took over 90 days to complete, 28% in 2020, up from 24% in 2019.
The Division's fastest disciplines (Firearms, Field Investigations, and Biology) completed 66%, 63%, and 51% in under 30 days, respectively, but they represent a much smaller percentage of the

Factors Affecting Results

postmortem toxicology made up nearly 20% of the total request volume, their total turnaround time will continue to impact the Division's overall KPM performance.  In addition, three new positions
were added by the Legislature to assist the Toxicology Unit and are anticipated to begin in early 2022.  This will provide some relief, but the training period will result in an initial decrease in
productivity.

How Are We Doing
The Oregon State Police’s ability to meet this performance measure solely depends on staff resources within the Criminal Justice Information Services (CJIS) Division.  Livescan technology and
automated processing improvements have significantly reduced that dependency and a revised target of 4-days became possible, down from the previous 8-day turnaround at initial implementation of
livescan statewide.
For calendar year 2020, the 4-day manual card turnaround target was met and exceeded.  The manual card average turnaround time for 2020 was 1.2 days (28.5 hours) and for the year, manual
cards represented 2% of the total criminal arrest cards received.  Automated submissions, representing 98% of the total criminal arrest cards received, were completed within 0.38 hours (46 minutes).
This is an improvement over the 2-hour target for this submission workload.
Factors Affecting Results
Staffing levels and agencies’ use of livescan technology have a direct effect on our performance results, as does the availability of our infrastructure systems.  All of Oregon’s county jail facilities use
livescan technology to submit their arrest fingerprint cards, with a growing number of local police departments also acquiring livescan technology.  All agencies using livescan devices submit
fingerprints using the automated process.  However, there continue to be instances where manually captured prints are necessary due to either an agency not having a livescan machine or
circumstances that make a manual print necessary (i.e. nature disaster, power issues etc.).  Therefore, OSP will continue to maintain some level of accepted manual processing services in order to
meet this need.
During this reporting period the Computerized Criminal History (CCH) and Automated Biometric Identification System (ABIS) Units both had vacancies, as well as new impacts brought on by COVID-
19.  While the ABIS Unit maintained their 24/7 scheduling and availability, the CCH Unit was largely sent home to telework in compliance with the Oregon State Police and the Department of
Administrative Services’ requirements.  Most importantly, contributing agencies significantly decreased their overall submissions, using cite and release for many months before resuming regular
arrest fingerprint-based bookings.  This helped CJIS maintain our core services while addressing internal resource issues that had not previously been encountered within the Division.

How Are We Doing
The 2020 wildland fire season was initially relatively quiet until an east wind event on Labor Day caused significant fire activity throughout the state; most notably on the west side of the Cascade
Mountains.  The Oregon Fire Mutual Aid System and The Office of State Fire Marshal (OSFM) did not have the capacity to fully staff and suppress the fires that were sparked or spread by that wind
event.  A statewide invocation of the Conflagration Act allowed OSFM to engage the statewide fire event more effectively.  In total, OSFM responded to 18 declared conflagrations.  Resources from
179 Oregon fire agencies were mobilized to protect threatened structures during the 2020 fire season at an estimated cost of $26 million to protect 172,427 structures with an estimated value of more
than $43 billion.  The success rate of protecting the structures threatened in the 18 conflagration zones during 2020 was 98.1 percent.
Factors Affecting Results
The primary factors affecting the results of this measure are the location of fire, the weather conditions of the area, the speed and effectiveness of the initial fire responses, and the determinations
made by the involved county’s Fire Defense Board Chief and local Incident Commander.  Ultimately, a variety of factors including the size and rate of advancement of the fire will be the determining
factor affecting the need for a conflagration request and therefore the results of this key performance measure.  Recent fires in California, Washington, Idaho and Montana have resulted in
catastrophic losses in the number of residences destroyed by the fast-moving wildland fires.  The hazards and vulnerabilities faced in these other states are similar to those in Oregon.
The target of saving 100 percent of threatened structures after the initiation of operations by OSFM mobilized resources at the scene of a conflagration is an honorable and lofty goal.  There are many
variables beyond the control of OSFM when confronting wildland fires.  Establishing and achieving results that approach or meet this target requires bold and aggressive actions by the responding
resources.  This is possible as a result of training, and the implementation of effective planning and tactical operations.  However, mitigating the impact of wildland fires in the areas surrounding
structures across Oregon is often beyond the control of OSFM.

As of September 2021, the residential fire death rate in Oregon for calendar year 2020 is unknown.  The Office of State Fire Marshal (OSFM) receives the unintentional residential fire death rate data
