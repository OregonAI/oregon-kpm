How Are We Doing
PERFORMANCE: The six-month Entered Employment Rate (EER) for State Fiscal Year (SFY) 2025 is 66%. This is three percentage points above the target of 63%.

OUR STRATEGY: Workforce Operations collaborates with our workforce partners to tailor our services to individuals’ needs. This helps prepare job seekers to plan and carry out successful job
searches, which improves employment outcomes.

ABOUT THE TARGET: Targets are negotiated directly between the U.S. Department of Labor and the Oregon Employment Department. A higher percentage is better.

HOW WE COMPARE: The performance in SFY 2025 was 66% compared to 68% in 2024 and 65% in 2023. The national average for SFY 2024 was 68% (national data for SFY 2025 is not yet
available). Of note, SFY 2024 regional performance varied across states with Alaska reporting 69%, Arizona 60%, California 60%, Hawaii 61%, Idaho 72%, and Washington 69%.

Factors Affecting Results
Labor market conditions have shifted. Oregon's unemployment rate was 4.8% in May 2025, which was up by 1.2 percentage points from the recent low of 3.6% in spring 2023. While 4.8% is still a
relatively low unemployment rate over the past four decades, it's relatively high for non-recessionary norms of the past decade. Oregon's unemployment rate in May 2025 was also slightly higher than
the 4.2% rate nationally. Job openings no longer outnumber the unemployed, with the unemployed-to-job vacancy ratio rising to 2-to-1 in SFY 2025, well above the U.S. (1-to-1).

Although the state’s overall EER of 66% exceeded its target, the rate for many groups facing barriers to employment was well below this mark. These groups include low-income (62%), displaced
homemakers (57%), houseless individuals (52%), individuals with disabilities (57%), and those experiencing re-entry after being in jail or prison (57%). Challenges to returning to work include lack of
current and transferable skills, employment gaps, transportation, childcare, housing stability, and other factors disproportionally affecting underserved and underrepresented communities.

We continue to provide reemployment services to job seekers in person and virtually, focusing on Unemployment Insurance claimants, migrant and seasonal farmworkers, Supplemental Nutrition
Assistance Program (SNAP) recipients, Veterans, and other targeted and underrepresented populations. Examples of reemployment services include virtual job-finding workshops; customized labor
market and career information; local resource rooms equipped with computers, phones, and photocopy machines; job fairs and networking events; and support services (transportation assistance,
work-related clothing, etc.) through the SNAP Training and Employment Program and other grants.

As part of our integrated service delivery approach, staff also work to connect job seekers with workforce services, including partner organizations that provide targeted populations such as dislocated
workers and youth with career development and skills training opportunities. One innovative example of this work includes outreach to the Deer Ridge Correctional Institution to provide one-on-one
assistance to adults in custody and connect them to a WorkSource Oregon center before release. Additional outreach is planned in SFY 2026 at the Columbia River Correctional Institution. Another
example is launching a new business model in 2023 to streamline and improve outreach efforts to connect migrant and seasonal farmworkers with workforce services and related programs. This
model, which includes additional full-time outreach staff and a newly created Migrant and Seasonal Farm Worker (MSFW) Program Coordinator position, has resulted in additional outreach contacts
and improved coordination with other service providers serving MSFWs. In SFY 2025, 35,885 MSFW outreach contacts were made (this is not a distinct count as individuals may be contacted more
than once).

We continue to invest in business services to support workforce investment activities and help local businesses through customized screening and recruitment efforts. This often involves site visits to
help build relationships with employers and better understand their workforce needs. This model has improved communication and coordination of service delivery between workforce partners and
allowed for more streamlined and targeted placement of WorkSource Oregon customers, particularly those facing barriers to employment.

How Are We Doing
PERFORMANCE: The 12-month Employment Retention Rate (ERR) for SFY 2025 is 68%. This is three percentage points above the target of 65%.

OUR STRATEGY: Workforce Operations collaborates with our workforce partners to tailor our services to individuals’ needs and address potential barriers to employment. This improves job seekers’
long-term employment outcomes.

ABOUT THE TARGET: Targets are negotiated directly between the U.S. Department of Labor and the Oregon Employment Department. A higher percentage is better.

HOW WE COMPARE: The performance was 68% in SFY 2025, matching the employment outcome for 2024. The national average for SFY 2024 was 69% (national data for SFY 2025 is not yet
available). Of note, SFY 2024 regional performance varied across states with Alaska reporting 69%, Arizona 59%, California 61%, Hawaii 62%, Idaho 70%, and Washington 69%.

Factors Affecting Results
Like KPM #1, this measure benefits from favorable labor market conditions. The SFY 2025 ERR of 68% exceeded its annual target in 2025, despite a gradual rise in seasonally adjusted
unemployment from 4.1% in June 2024 to 4.8% in May 2025.

Although the state’s overall ERR of 68% exceeded its target, the rate for many groups facing barriers to employment was well below this mark. These groups include low-income (63%), long-term
unemployed (62%), displaced homemakers (58%), houseless individuals (64%), individuals with disabilities (58%), and those experiencing re-entry after being in jail or prison (55%).
To address the reemployment needs of those facing barriers, we use a braided service delivery strategy. This involves identifying an individual’s needs and co-enrolling them in workforce programs
they may be eligible to join. These programs include SNAP Training and Employment, Trade Adjustment Assistance for Workers, Workforce Innovation and Opportunity Act Title 1 Adult and
Dislocated Worker, and National Farmworkers Jobs Program. These programs complement our core labor exchange and job-matching services through case management, re-training, financial
assistance with transportation, tools, clothing, and other job-related expenses, and follow-up (retention) services to support individuals who have entered employment.

A strong alignment between the employer’s needs and the job seeker's interests and skills improves job retention. To this end, the agency employs several approaches. These include meeting on-site
with employers as part of the recruitment process to learn about their workforce needs; helping employers establish LinkedIn accounts to match to and network with WorkSource Oregon candidates
through an online recruiter platform; using job developers to help connect job seekers facing barriers to employment with businesses; and helping improve the employability of job seekers through the
identification of job readiness skills in areas such as interviewing, resumes, soft skills, and career development.

How Are We Doing
PERFORMANCE: There were 92,849 placements in SFY 2025 and expenditures in Workforce Operations were $74,046,368. This translates to a cost per placement of $797, which is 77% higher
(worse) than the target. On a positive note, the cost per placement decreased for the third consecutive year since reaching a high of $2,899 in SFY 2022. (SFY 2022 performance was significantly
impacted by the pandemic, with only 18,541 placements)

OUR STRATEGY: Workforce Operations continues to monitor budgetary constraints and fiscal responsibility. We continue to work with our WorkSource Oregon partners to leverage resources, reduce
costs, and address changing customer needs while seeking to improve outcomes across the state.

ABOUT THE TARGET: Cost per placement measures the cost of the program each fiscal year. The targets will be adjusted for inflation each biennium by a maximum of 4%. A lower cost is better.

HOW WE COMPARE: The cost per placement decreased by 18% between SFY 2025 and SFY 2024, when it was $974. Cost per placement fell by $2,092 (-72%) since SFY 2022 when it reached
$2,889. There is not an equivolent national measure.

Factors Affecting Results
Placements are a direct result of the number of individuals served by staff in WorkSource Oregon centers. Simply put, fewer people using the centers impacts placements. While individuals receiving
services have increased each year since reaching a record low in SFY 2021 during the pandemic, they remain below pre-pandemic levels. As a comparison, we served 122,374 individuals in SFY
2019 compared to 109,586 in SFY 2025, a 10% decrease.
Several factors may explain fewer customers being served at WorkSource Oregon centers. First, competition from public job boards and other online resources have increased in recent years with the
rise in artificial intelligence and data analytics. Second, the agency’s aging computer system for the delivery of labor-exchange services has not kept pace with technology, limiting the system’s ability
to meet the needs of job seekers and promote reengagement. Third, a generation raised in a largely digital age with access to smartphones and other technology to search for employment may be
less inclined to register with the public labor exchange. Fourth, the number of customers served is heavily influenced by the number of Unemployment Insurance claims filed, as unattached claimants
(those required to seek work) must meet with employment services staff in the WSO centers to be eligible for benefits. The number of claimants attending their required meeting in SFY 2024 was
59,268 compared to 54,663 in SFY 2025, a decrease of almost 8%. In the end, it’s difficult to pinpoint the exact cause of this decline and the agency will explore this trend further to better understand
how economic and demographic trends and other factors may impact this number.

To help contain costs, we implemented a statewide service delivery model in March 2023. This model allows staff across the state to serve job seekers by video conference regardless of customer
location, thus increasing operational efficiency. Additional measures include working closely with our workforce partners to leverage resources through shared facilities, and a strategic business model
that emphasizes co-enrollment across workforce programs where appropriate.

To help job seekers find work, our business processes have and will continue to adapt. This includes a strong focus on helping underrepresented and vulnerable populations such as Unemployment
Insurance claimants, long-term unemployed, SNAP recipients, migrant and seasonal farmworkers, and Veterans. This service delivery strategy aligns with federal expectations that workforce
programs provide a higher level of intensive, customized service to individuals facing barriers. It’s important to note that this work often requires more time and resources, thus increasing service
delivery costs.

And finally, we will continue working with state and local workforce partners to effectively address the labor needs of businesses and to connect job seekers to available employment opportunities and
leverage resources. We have had success providing customized services to employers and tailoring job seeker services to meet local demand. This approach continues to produce high levels of
employer satisfaction and demand continues to grow. To meet this demand, business services representatives are focusing on job development and other placement strategies such as partnering with
LinkedIn to connect job seekers with employment opportunities through an online recruiter platform, hosting job fairs and open houses, using a standardized set of criteria to assess an individual’s
work readiness skills to increase their chances of being a successful candidate, and pairing work-ready candidates with career development coordinators who help them achieve their career goals
through one-on-one coaching and connecting them to on-the-job training and employment opportunities.

How Are We Doing
PERFORMANCE: The timeliness of first payments was 83% in SFY 2025, which is lower (worse) than the target of 89%.

OUR STRATEGY: The Unemployment Insurance Program pursues greater efficiency by streamlining UI processes and adopting new technologies that improve timeliness and the customer
experience. We continue hiring and training employees, improving the employee training process, and updating our support tools. We are monitoring performance metrics to anticipate when and
where reallocation of resources can improve performance.

ABOUT THE TARGET: The 89% target for this measurement was established in the 2019-21 biennium. This is 2% higher than the US Department of Labor (DOL) target. The percentage of claims
paid within 21 days of the initial claim filing reflects the efficiency of determining eligibility and giving unemployed workers their first benefit payment. A higher percentage of timely first payments is
better.

HOW WE COMPARE: The measure ranged from 42% to 93% between SFY 2019 and SFY 2024. The performance in SFY 2024 of 73% was below the average national performance of 79%. The
performance in SFY 2025 of 83% matched the average national performance of 83%.

Factors Affecting Results
We continue to prioritize timely benefit payments. Chronic federal underfunding makes it a challenge to meet this measure.

Processing initial claims within four to seven days of filing positively affects first-pay timeliness, as does resolving 80% of issues on claims within 14 days. High call volume and backlogs in
administrative appeals of our decisions negatively affect first-pay timeliness. The transition from legacy computer systems to the modernized Frances system presented additional challenges, which
resulted in service delays as employees and customers adapted and gained proficiency using the new system.

We continue to develop comprehensive reporting dashboards in the modernized system to better measure and monitor how quickly we process initial claims, call volume, non-monetary
determinations, suspended claims, and investigation workloads. We are also updating reporting dashboards specific to first-pay timeliness to measure and monitor the workloads that significantly
affect the metric and ensure we are correctly prioritizing those workloads. The process changes and efficiencies gained through modernization have improved visibility into the workload, resulted in a
10% improvement in this metric over the past year, and are anticipated to continue mitigating some of the negative impacts on this metric in the coming years.

How Are We Doing
PERFORMANCE: The percentage of timely non-monetary determinations was 77% in SFY 2025, falling short of its 80% target.

OUR STRATEGY: The Unemployment Insurance Program continues seeking efficiencies through the modernized Frances system and related process improvements. This will result in improved
timeliness without adversely impacting other customer service standards, including developing new tools and reviewing and revising production standards. Telephone system capabilities are being

Factors Affecting Results
Declining federal funding led us to reduce staffing levels, which decreased performance. We have reduced the number of staff since the end of the COVID-19 pandemic, and the complexity of laws
has continued to increase. Due to the reduction in staff, it has been and continues to be a challenge to balance the need to make determinations quickly and have as complete information as possible
to minimize fraud, potential overpayments, and improper denials of benefits.
Pandemic conditions resulted in historically low performance for non-monetary timeliness, and long-term pressures from added program requirements and inadequate federal funding continue to be a
challenge for meeting the target in this measure. Until March 2024, outdated technology contributed to our challenges by significantly limiting opportunities to shift work processes or adapt to shifting
workloads. On March 4, 2024, we launched our modernized business processes and technology systems for UI programs. After an anticipated, and brief, decline in performance after launch during
which staff gained knowledge and proficiency using the new system, our overall performance in this area has begun to improve, including a 21% improvement over the past year. While modernization
is a multiyear endeavor, the modernized system and processes allow for more automation and upfront fact-finding, improving the timeliness of non-monetary determinations.

How Are We Doing
PERFORMANCE: Controllable administrative costs were $114,931,962 and benefit payments were $920,474,225 in SFY 2025 resulting in cost-relative-to-benefits-paid ratio of 12%. This is higher
(worse) than the target of 10%.

OUR STRATEGY: The Unemployment Insurance Program continues pursuing efficiencies from centralization and new technology to streamline UI processes to improve timeliness and customer
service. This focuses in part on expanding the ways the public can easily use self-service tools to get information that traditionally required staff involvement.

ABOUT THE TARGET: The target for this measurement was 10% in the 2017 Legislative Session. As a newer measurement, there was minimal historical data to set this target. The cost-divided-by-
benefits-paid ratio reflects the efficiency of making benefit payments. A lower percentage is better.

HOW WE COMPARE: The 12% measure in SFY 2025 was one percentage point lower than SFY 2024 and four percentage points lower than SFY 2023. The measure was 12% in 2018 and 2019, 3%
in 2020 and 2021, and 11% in 2022. There is not a national measure compiled for comparison.

Factors Affecting Results
In our experience, administrative costs, as a percent of benefits paid, tend to be lower (better) during recessionary periods and higher (worse) in a strong economy. During a recession, benefits paid
increase faster than administrative costs because more people claim more benefits for a longer period.
The low ratios in 2020 and 2021 were due to the CARES Act, Continued Assistance Act (CAA), and American Rescue Plan Act (ARPA). These created new federal benefit programs that increased
benefit payments: Federal Pandemic Unemployment Compensation (FPUC) and Pandemic Emergency Unemployment Compensation (PEUC). However, as the economy improved and fewer
benefits were paid out, this measure has moved toward the historical average. The current higher ratio represents investments in building skills, tools, and infrastructure that will ultimately increase
efficiency. In addition to our modernized Unemployment Insurance (UI) and Paid Leave system, examples of these investments include updating performance measures and creating detailed standard
operating procedures for the modernized UI system, re-establishing performance dashboards that allow program leadership to track the overall health and direction of the program, continuous
improvement to UI benefit letters, developing modular self-directed UI training that managers can assign and team members can access individually, and reviewing and modernizing UI benefit
eligibility rules.

We have modernized the UI system. The modernized UI contributions systems went live in September 2022 and the modernized UI benefits systems went live in March 2024. The new system offers
many self-service options and automates many manual processes. We anticipated and realized an initial decrease in productivity as team members learned the new system before we saw productivity
gains. We continue to work on system operations and enhancements to improve and automate many processes that have been manual in our legacy system.

Automating claims processes and modernizing the technical and business environments will improve staff efficiency, allowing increased opportunities to improve each customer’s experience
navigating the UI program. We remain focused on increasing self-service options for the public to improve customer service.

How Are We Doing
PERFORMANCE: The percentage of timely UI appeals was 25% in SFY 2024, which is below (worse than) the target of 60%.

OUR STRATEGY: The Office of Administrative Hearings (OAH) has reallocated resources to hire more Limited Duration Unemployment Insurance Administrative Law Judges (ALJs) to address the
record number of Unemployment Insurance (UI) contested case referrals. In addition, OAH will organize “UI Weeks” when the entire agency will exclusively focus on UI work, with the goal of clearing
1,000 total contest cases per week. As these efforts ramp up, we have assigned Senior ALJs to take on UI work and rotated ALJs from other teams to UI. These changes have already reduced the
“Ready to Schedule” contested case queue by thousands of cases.

We will continue to work older contested cases, which will negatively affect this metric until we push through the backlog. We anticipate this work will be completed by late 2026.

ABOUT THE TARGETS: For UI cases, timeliness is defined as the percentage of cases in which a party has requested a hearing that are heard or otherwise resolved within 30 days of a hearing
request. The target of 60% is the target established by the U.S. Department of Labor. A higher percentage is better.

HOW WE COMPARE: SFY 2025 performance was 25%, significantly above the 10% reached in SFY 2023 but below the 34% achieved in SFY 2024. The measure varied from 27% to 83% during
SFY 2018-2024.

Factors Affecting Results
Since the pandemic, OAH has overcome several challenges related to the UI caseload. At its apex in October 2022, OAH’s UI program had 10,709 contested case referrals in the pandemic backlog.
This was in addition to record levels of cases being referred on a weekly basis. Monumental efforts were undertaken to clear this body of work, including a doubling of the size of the UI ALJ bench.
ALJs were reassigned from other teams and UI work was assigned during gaps in ALJ schedules. Over the course of the biennium, the OAH was able to completely clear the backlog.

However, OED’s launch of UI benefits in Frances streamlined the lower appeals process and increased the overall volume of contested casework. While this was initially thought to be a temporary
phenomenon, it has become a permanent condition. Contested case referrals have broken records for OAH yet again, resulting in an overall 117.97% increase in case volume. Weekly contested case
referrals now routinely exceed pandemic highs.

Even with the increased size of the UI bench, OAH has struggled to keep up with the pace of work. In response, the agency has begun an additional round of hiring after reallocating resources from
other program areas. OAH has continued to leverage retirees and rotations from other program areas. Senior ALJs have stepped up to take on cases whenever possible. UI ALJs are working
extremely busy schedules with heavy caseloads, hearing 24 contested cases per week. These efforts have already made an impact, reducing the “Ready to Schedule” contested case queue from
6,149 cases in October 2024 to 3,002 cases in September 2025.

OAH will conduct “UI Weeks” beginning in September 2025. During this time, all six ALJ panels will exclusively take on UI contested case work. Managers and the Chief ALJ will also conduct
hearings. The goal will be to conduct 1,000 contested cases per ”UI Week.” While this will negatively impact other program areas, it is a necessary step to address the number of referrals.

The reality is that conducting the work necessary for improving KPM #7 negatively impacts the metric. Every contested case scheduled and worked outside of the target drags down the average.
However, it is imperative that we schedule those cases where the participants have been waiting the longest for a hearing. With the combination of additional hiring and UI weeks, the OAH is
anticipating positive progress over the next year despite limited resources and significant challenges.
