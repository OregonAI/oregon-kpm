How Are We Doing
This is a new KPM for the Office of Aging and People with Disabilities so there is no data available to report for the current year. APD created and implemented a new tracking tool within our case
management system that will allow us to track timeliness of LTSS eligibility determinations. The tool was launched in February 2025 and will provide data to set the baseline and progress toward the

Factors Affecting Results
Moving financial eligibility into the ONE system stopped our ability to track this measure. The new tracking tool is now producing service timeliness data and will be the primary source for reporting. A

workloads.   Untimely eligibility determinations may delay hospital discharges, jeopardize placement in long term care facilities or postpone needed in-home or community-based services to remain
safe and stable.

How Are We Doing
APD continues to make progress in ensuring access to paid caregiver services for older adults and people with disabilities. In January 2024, 85.5% of eligible consumers had access to paid
caregivers. By January 2025, this increased to 87.7%, reflecting steady improvement toward the 90% target.

are organized on a calendar year basis (Jan-Dec) but going forward will align with State Fiscal Years.
Factors Affecting Results
This new KPM provides a broader and more meaningful picture than the former Nursing Facility KPM, showing whether sufficient caregivers are available across settings to meet the needs of eligible
consumers. The upward trend demonstrates improvement, and the expansion of the provider workforce following the COVID-19 pandemic has been a contributing factor. Growth in the Homecare
Worker and Community-Based Care provider workforce post-COVID has helped increase accessibility and supported progress toward the 90% target.
Workforce recruitment efforts for Homecare Workers and Adult Foster Homes (AFHs) are starting to result in positive trends. Additionally, APD expects the new rate methodologies for AFHs, and
Residential Care Facilities should help expand additional capacity

Factors Affecting Results
During this reporting period, there were 19,027 investigations completed by APS (compared with 18,729 from the past reporting period, an increase of 1.6%). Of those, 18,785 (98.7%) received a

During this 2025 reporting period, there were 1,449 Nursing Facility Complaint Intakes due for Investigations (compared with 1241 of those from the 2024 reporting period, a 0.86% increase, 297
(20.5%) received a timely response. The data reported during the last reporting period was transcribed incorrectly which accounts for the wide variation in response timeliness. The Centers for
Medicare and Medicaid Services (CMS) requires the Nursing Facility Survey Unit (NFSU) to complete work in a prioritized Tiered system which is directed by CMS and sent annually to the NFSU.
CMS requires NFSU  to prioritize and complete Tier 1 at 100% prior to completing Tiers 2, 3 and 4 work. Tiers 2,3,4 work may be concurrent with Tier 1 work, however it must be completed in the
priority sequence. Tier 1 work includes intakes triaged at immediate jeopardy, a 3-7 business day response, recertification surveys, Special Focus Facility monitoring requirements and any additional
requirement(s) set by CMS. Tier 2 work is non-immediate jeopardy high intakes, requiring up to an 18 business-day response but not to average more than 15 business days, as well as any additional
requirement(s) set by CMS. Tier 3 work is non-immediate jeopardy medium which is 45 calendar days. However, CMS does not track these intakes and often changes between 45 calendar days or
they need conducted on the next onsite visit. Tier 4 work is non-immediate jeopardy low intakes which are only tracked and trended and have no timeliness standards. These standards impact timely
reporting on this measure.

How Are We Doing
The outcome of 11.6% is Federal Fiscal Year 2024 data, for Report year 2025.This is the first year that this measure is being reported as a KPM for Child Welfare, and it is a federal measure.
Factors Affecting Results
There is ongoing cross-program analysis of root cause issues impacting this key performance measure.  Oregon statute requires a CPS assessment for all reports of abuse, including for each incident
of abuse reported. This at times results in multiple reports for the same child victim who is disclosing ongoing historical abuse by the same person. In the data this will look like the child has
experienced re-abuse, however the abuse reported in the second instance occurred prior to the initial report.  There have been technological updates to the electronic information system to record
‘incident dates’ based on information gathered during the CPS assessment that may improve the accuracy of this measure.  Other factors affecting this measure include a lack of access to
preventative and therapeutic services for children with exceptional behavioral and mental health needs and the community’s reliance on Oregon’s child welfare systems to address family conditions
when there are gaps in available resources. Oregon continues to work with community and staff to educate on poverty versus neglect as they are often confused, resulting in overreporting to the child
abuse hotline. ODHS is working to align service systems to identify families in need of supportive services rather than protective services. Child Welfare leadership continues to focus on community
engagement to improve access to resources and services and prevent child abuse. Through Continuous Quality Improvement cycles, local offices are continuing to address sufficiency of information
gathering during the child protective service assessment to accurately identify safety threats and the development of safety plans that help ensure children are not subject to another occurrence of
abuse. The Safety and Permanency programs are part of this planning and are providing coaching and support. Oregon child welfare is enhancing the training and support of the child welfare
workforce for substance use disorder through staff education and staged implementation. The increased knowledge and skills needed to understand how substance use disorder impacts family
functioning and child safety will improve the effectiveness of child welfare interventions reducing the reoccurrence of child abuse and neglect.

How Are We Doing
The outcome for each section is Federal Fiscal Year 2024 data, for Report Year 2025 (October 2023-September 2024).  This is the first year of reporting this data as a KPM.
Reunification
In FFY 2024, Oregon improved its performance by 3.45 percentage points from FFY 2023. Oregon’s performance of 79.75 % of foster children reunifying within 12 months of foster care entry is the
best performance in at least 10 years. While this does not meet the new target goal of 85% this is a significant improvement.
Guardianship
Timeliness to guardianship was added to this KPM for 2025. The actual performance did not meet the target measure. A workgroup and sub-groups were initiated in 2023 and concluded in 2024. The
workgroup members (internal and external to ODHS) assessed and made recommendations to improve guardianship processes. Changes to administrative rules, procedures, forms, and trainings in
order to support implementation of those recommendations have or will soon be implemented. Examples of improvements include updates to the guardianship addendum (study/assessment to
consider whether a caregiver should be the guardian resource for a child) and providing trainings regarding permanency committees (which includes committees to consider the plan and placement
resource for guardianship). The goal is to improve Child Welfare staff’s understanding and implementation of guardianship processes so that guardianships can be achieved more timely and in ways
that best meet the needs of young people in care.
Adoption
Timeliness to adoption
was added to this KPM for 2025. Previously the KPM related to adoption was KPM #6 “Timely Adoption Once Children Are Legally Free”. That target was surpassed for the last
three years. The actual performance for the adoption section of this revised KPM for 2025 is slightly more than three percentage points lower than the target. ODHS has less influence in timeliness to
adoption for the period prior to a child becoming legally free for adoption as those timelines are impacted by variables for parental engagement and progress and by court processes, including length
of time to achieve involuntary termination of parental rights and appeals of court orders. Child Welfare will continue improvement efforts outlined in reporting for prior KPM #6, assessing ways to
improve concurrent planning, and implementing updated assessment processes for potential adoptive resources.
Factors Affecting Results
Oregon has continued to enhance reunification efforts by focusing on engagement of families and community in child welfare safety planning and case planning. The Family Preservation and
Reunification Program has coordinated efforts with the Continuous Quality Improvement (CQI) program, ODHS local branch staff and leadership to improve timely completion of case plans, increase
the percentage of monthly face-to-face contacts of caseworkers with parents and improve the quality of ongoing safety plans. In June 2024, the exception to having face-to-face contacts with parents
was removed from rule, procedure and associated data reports were updated. This change was made in an effort for Child Welfare to emphasize the crucial impact consistent face-to-face contact with
parents has for families and case planning, improve timeliness to permanency for children, and have Child Welfare data better reflect actual practice. Improvements in timeliness to Reunification, and
subsequent timeliness to implementing the concurrent plan when reunification cannot be achieved, increases the likelihood of improvements in timeliness for achieving Guardianship and Adoption
plans.   Recent implementation of the “Relative Pathway” is expected to improve outcomes for children, including those for early and ongoing placement with relatives and timeliness to permanency.
The new approaches, processes, forms, and training curriculum for assessment of relatives for certification and adoption (including new relative pathway adoption addendum) are expected to improve
family engagement and support for parents in the reunification process. It should also positively impact timeliness to achieve guardianship and adoption when reunification cannot be achieved,
especially when the guardian or adoptive resource is a relative.

How Are We Doing
This metric applies to substantiated abuse of adults residing in licensed and endorsed programs. Because these programs have sought licenses or endorsements, ODDS can have a direct impact on
the provider agencies involved in abuse and neglect.
Most reports of abuse and neglect involved licensed settings. Employees in these settings are mandatory reporters of abuse and are trained to recognize signs of abuse. The adult population receiving
services in these settings increased 4.1% from 2023 (8921 individuals) to 2024 (9291 individuals). During the same period the number of adults with a substantiated abuse allegation rose from 139 to
173, a 24.5% increase. Thus, the estimate of abuse for this population increased by 0.30% over the previous year.
Because of a lack of national abuse data, it is a challenge to establish a meaningful IDD abuse rate target. What data are available show that developmentally disabled individuals are much more
likely to be the victims of violent crime and domestic violence. Oregon targets are currently based on trends of historical data.
Oregon’s Centralized Abuse Management (CAM) information system is providing enhanced tracking and monitoring of abuse referrals and investigations in the state. The CAM system is being
constantly monitored and improved to enhance access and availability of information across the state and greatly improve the ability to make data informed decisions.
Factors Affecting Results
The last five years have seen significant variation in the substantiation rates for adults with developmental disabilities including a significant drop in substantiations associated with the COVID
pandemic. While any increase in abuse rates is troubling it is important to note that abuse rates aren’t increasing at the same pace as the population receiving services. This year the rate increased to
1.86%, slightly above the long-term target (1.7%). Taken together the last 5 years has shown a slow increase towards pre-COVID rates, with 2024 reaching the highest level since the beginning of the
pandemic.
Abuse rates for people with intellectual/developmental disabilities can be affected by many factors including:
The high acuity and service needs of residents being served in community-based care settings.
High turnover rate of treatment and support staff in all settings.
An adult’s right to make decisions about their living situation, companions, etc.
Barriers to the reporting of abuse by cognitively impaired clients.
Limited resources available to respond to and support people with intellectual/developmental disabilities who are abused (e.g. domestic violence shelters, counseling resources, etc.).
What needs to be done in this area includes:
Ongoing training for service coordinators, personal agents, personal support workers, direct support providers, service providers and facility staff in recognizing, reporting, and preventing abuse.
Research and collaboration with community response systems and resources, including domestic violence interventions, sexual assault response, mental health services, housing, etc.
Coordination and participation with local area multidisciplinary teams and coordinated-care organizations.

How Are We Doing
Guided by the Oregon Department of Human Services (ODHS) Equity North Star [1], the Office of Self-Sufficiency Programs (SSP) works toward a future where all who live in Oregon have the
resources and support they need to achieve whole well-being for themselves, their families, and their communities—regardless of race, identity, or place.
Our commitment to this vision is reflected in supports and services aiming to improve the economic stability of the communities we serve. Achieving living wages is essential for maximizing human
potential. Families whose incomes are near or below the federal poverty level often struggle to afford basic needs such as food, housing, utilities, childcare, and clothing. This measure tracks the
median percentage of participants who earn at or above living wages four quarters after exiting any Self-Sufficiency program.
This measure is based on data provided by the Oregon Employment Department (OED), which tracks participant earnings one year after exiting all SSP programs. Only those whose incomes are
reported to Oregon Employment Department (OED) are included in this analysis, which excludes people employed in the informal economy. Whether earnings reflect a living wage is determined by
comparing earnings to the Washington State University Self-Sufficiency Standard for Oregon, which outlines income levels required to meet basic needs, with adjustments for family size and
geographic location. [2]
The 2025 results reflect participants who left SSP programs in State Fiscal Year 2023. The most recent data show that 72.9% of participants have been earning living wages after exiting SSP
programs. Performance on this measure tracked closely to last year’s result of 75.6%, marking a stabilization of the pronounced upward trend following pandemic lows.
[1] https://www.oregon.gov/odhs/equity/Documents/odhs-equity-north-star.pdf
[2] The Self-Sufficiency Standard for Oregon 2024, Center for Women’s Welfare, University of Washington. Available online at: http://selfsufficiencystandard.org/oregon
Factors Affecting Results
The stabilization trend for households earning a living wage may be attributed to several factors including economic conditions, the status of the labor market, and macroeconomic trends such as

Renewals processed timely remain below the 95% target overall, with aggregate performance at 91.9% in 2025 to date. Performance is mixed across programs: Medical renewals are well above
target, signaling strong process design and workforce familiarity with this caseload. SNAP, ERDC, and TANF renewals continue to lag, reflecting challenges in client responsiveness, workload
prioritization, and the coordination of renewal notices with participant engagement. While improvements have been made, these results reinforce the need for ongoing focus on consistency across
program areas to prevent breaks in benefits and ensure program integrity.
Factors Affecting Results
Renewal timeliness is influenced by the interaction between participants and staff. Households may not respond to requests for information or return renewal forms on time, increasing the risk of
delayed processing. Staffing shortages, high turnover, and training gaps also contribute to variability in performance, particularly in complex programs. In addition, external factors such as mail delays
or client mobility can create barriers for timely submission of required documentation. Investment in outreach, participant education, and simplified renewal processes will be essential to meeting
targets.

How Are We Doing
SNAP accuracy continues a strong upward trajectory, improving from 77.1% in 2022 to 86.6% in 2025 to date. This improvement has contributed directly to the reduction in Oregon’s federally reported
Payment Error Rate (PER), which dropped from nearly 23% in 2022 to 13.4% in 2025. While the gains are substantial and reflect a culture shift toward accuracy and accountability, the accuracy rate
remains below the 94% target. Continued emphasis on training, case review feedback, and error trend monitoring will be required to sustain progress and close the remaining gap.

SNAP payment error rates have declined significantly over the past several years, improving from 22.99% in 2022 to 13.41% in 2025 to date. This steady reduction directly reflects the improvement in
SNAP application accuracy rate, highlighting the impacts from progress in SNAP application accuracy.
Factors Affecting Results
SNAP accuracy is affected by the complexity of federal policy, frequent program adjustments, and the wide range of income and household circumstances presented by participants. Staff must
interpret and apply policies consistently, often under time pressure. Client misreporting or incomplete reporting, especially around earned income, remains one of the leading drivers of error. Systems
functionality and clarity of eligibility rules in the ONE system are also central to ensuring correct determinations. Investments in targeted training, continuous quality assurance feedback loops, and
improved integration of policy guidance into system supports have been critical in driving accuracy gains to date and remain key factors in sustaining improvement.
