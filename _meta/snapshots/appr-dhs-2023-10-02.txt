How Are We Doing
In 2023, only 2.68% of Oregonians 65 or older needed assistance with publicly funded long-term care. This continues a noticeable downward trend that exceeds legislative targets.
Factors Affecting Results
The success of the AAA network administering Oregon Project Independence, Older Americans Act programs and the Aging and Disability Resource Connection contribute towards keeping older

system’s impact on older adults.  APD will continue to analyze the factors that are decreasing the utilization of publicly funded long term care.

How Are We Doing
APD’s performance in 2023 slightly behind the goals established.  Recent challenges related to workforce across the home and community- based care continuum have led to many individuals staying
for longer periods of time in nursing facilities, however we suspect that continued fears associated with COVID have led to a decline in nursing facility admissions.  Additionally, statewide policies have
focused on enhancing Medicaid in-home services and supports which may increase in-home options for individuals.  The Legislature authorized innovative programs in the 2021 Legislative Session.
The expansion of Oregon Project Independence through Medicaid, once approved by the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services, should result in more individuals with higher incomes and
assets receiving services in their own home delaying entry into higher levels of care.
Affordability for private pay individuals in any setting is challenging. Adult Foster Home rates and rates provided to in-home care agencies lag behind comparable systems, straining providers’ ability to
serve Medicaid individuals in home and community-based care settings.  Workforce challenges, with low wages for direct care workers continues to stress the system.  Medicaid rates, if too low,
increase private pay rates and decrease the capacity in home and community-based-care options.
Factors Affecting Results
Throughout the pandemic, we have learned, that in most cases, hospitals prefer to discharge older adults and people with disabilities needing additional care to nursing facilities. Institutional care may
be appropriate for certain individuals for short periods of time. However, alternatives such as reviewing insurance coverage for Home Health and establishing more intensive supports in other settings
could help support the state in continuing to reach this KPM.  For Medicaid consumers, DHS must continue to proactively ensure that older adults and people with disabilities are appropriately
transitioned from nursing facilities when their care can be supported in less restrictive and less costly settings. Doing this will allow DHS to continue meeting our targets. APD also needs to focus
efforts on developing new adult foster homes, addressing the workforce shortage across the long-term care continuum, and preserving our existing provider base.

How Are We Doing
As was the case last year, performance on this measure is below the target goal of 95% but has slightly increased from the last two years’ performance.
Factors Affecting Results
This measure includes a wide variety of assigned response times and two investigative entities.
Community Investigations completed by Adult Protective Services have response times of same day, end of next business day, or within 5 business days.
Facility (Adult Foster Home, Assisted Living Facility, Residential Care Facility, Memory Care) Investigations completed by Adult Protective Services have response times of same day and end of
next business day.
Nursing Facility investigations completed by the Nursing Facility Licensing Unit within Safety, Oversight and Quality have assigned timelines of two days or ten days.
During this reporting period, there were 18,633 investigations completed by APS (compared with 16,366 from the past reporting period, an increase of 13.9%). Of those, 18,317 (98.3%) received a
timely response. This is a slight decrease from 98.6% during the last reporting period.
During this reporting period, there were 1,411 Nursing Facility Investigations (compared with 1,713 from the past reporting period, a decrease of 17.6%). Of those, 391 (27.7%) received a timely
response. This is a significant increase from 8.8% during the last reporting period.
On March 4, 2020, when the COVID-19 pandemic started, CMS paused all states’ ability to conduct recertification and complaint investigations except for the highest priority complaints and focused
on infection control measures and oversight.  By November 12, 2021, NFSU had accumulated a backlog of approximately 496 complaints when CMS allowed states to resume investigation of all
pending complaints and resume conducting recertification surveys.  At that time, all the pending backlog complaints had exceeded the investigative timelines required by CMS. NFSU was required by
CMS to develop and implement a Corrective Action Plan that CMS has been monitoring quarterly via the State Performance Standards System (SPSS).  As of September 25, 2023, the remaining
backlog for NFSU has been reduced to approximately 182 complaints (decreasing our total backlog by 63.31%), and it has met the CMS expectations related to decreasing the complaint and
recertification survey backlog. NFSU continues to prioritize investigating the highest priority complaints and has met the CMS investigation timelines for those complaints.
Additionally, turnover, recruitment and training have presented several challenges for the NFSU. The work the NF Survey team does has been particularly hard throughout the public health
emergency, resulting in Surveyors retiring or moving to other positions. Recruitment has been taking longer because the NF Survey team is competing with many other entities for candidates who
meet the qualifications and have the necessary experience, particularly RN’s. Surveyor wages are not competitive with the current market wage for RNs. When a new NF Surveyor is hired, it takes
months for a new Surveyor to complete the required training to meet federal standards and be able to work more independently. Recruitment strategies are being evaluated such as: streamlining and
identifying process improvement opportunities; reviewing classification and compensation to be able to recruit competitively for RNs; and promoting professional development for current APD staff
interested in promotional career growth.

How Are We Doing
In FFY 2022, Oregon increased by 2.8 percentage points in this measure. This is a significant improvement over the prior two years when there was a decline in the percentage of foster children
exiting to reunification within 12 months of foster care entry. Oregon’s performance of 75.3 percent is less than 1 point from the target goal of 76 percent.

Factors Affecting Results
A couple of factors that may have negatively impacted the reunification rate of children in foster care during the previous two years has changed or improved. The significant drop in the number of

worker across the state allowing more time for workers to engage parents and implement reunification plans.
Over the last two years, Oregon has continued to enhance reunification efforts by significantly improving the timeliness of case plans within 60 days of a child entering foster care and steadily
increasing the percentage of monthly face-to-face contacts of caseworkers with parents. The Permanency Quality Assurance Reviews that are conducted twice a year have also indicated an
improvement in the quality of written Ongoing Safety Plans and Conditions for Return.

How Are We Doing
The outcome of 22.2% is Federal Fiscal Year 2022 data for report year 2023. This data point indicates Oregon continues to hold steady on the number of children served in the parental home for FFY
2022. The implementation of the Family Preservation approach with the support of the Family First Act began in March 2022. During this reporting period the approach was being developed in three
branches across the state for the latter 6 months so the impact felt in the overall percentages for this measure would be minimal. Oregon expects to see an increase in children served in home for FFY
2023 as the preservation approach in combination with supportive evidence-based services through Family First are slowly implemented in a phased approach across the state in partnership with the
Self Sufficiency Program. There has been clear feedback from families that they are experiencing the agency in a different more supportive and trauma-informed way. Staff have stated the
collaboration across programs opens more supports and resources for families for in-home safety planning as well as supporting reunification. Intentional, and strategic efforts to reach out to
communities and community partners by listening, repairing relationships and repurposing funding to support their efforts in local communities has built trust and strengthened and grown services and
increased concrete and economic supports for families. There also continues to be a focus on putting the Well-being Together Initiative and the Vision for Transformation into action on a local level by
strengthening relationships across ODHS programs and within the community and individual families ODHS is serving.
Factors Affecting Results
Oregon continues efforts to reach out and collaborate with communities and community partners to come together to develop resources throughout the state to support family stability which leads to
children remaining safely in their homes while working with ODHS or returning home quickly after being placed in substitute care. Oregon continues to focus on family engagement, concrete
connections to networks of support and engaging natural supports for families to manage safety threats while children are in-home. The Family Preservation approach being developed over the six
months of this reporting period in the three demonstration branches has focused on the foundations of in-home work. This includes writing guidance and procedures, strengthening practice and
integrating the work of the Self Sufficiency Program with the work of Child Welfare with the goal of seamless supports for families. This foundational work was imperative to setting the stage for
innovations that are predicted to support an increase in the number of in-home cases. It will take time to see these impacts reflected in the data. As the preservation approach moves across the state
and real-time feedback informs a final “model” it should result in short- and long-term outcomes supporting families being together. Case reviews continue to indicate in-home cases are often worked
by protective services workers instead of these cases being transitioned to permanency/preservation workers which can result in less intensive support for families in their homes, cases closed prior to
the family conditions being fully addressed, staff seeing families in home on an infrequent basis or insufficient safety planning resulting in removal and placement in foster care.  The family
preservation program hopes to address each of these areas.
Placement of children in foster care is the most restrictive and least desirable outcome to manage child safety. Whenever possible, children who can remain with their family with an in-home safety
plan or be safety reunified with their family will continue to receive services in-home. Currently, Oregon’s practice for offering in-home cases is inconsistent around the state. The Department believes
the implementation of Family Preservation and the services funded through the Family First Act will counter this inconsistency and offer specific guidance and support for workers and families to
increase in-home cases.

How Are We Doing
This metric applies to substantiated abuse of adults in licensed and endorsed programs. Because these programs have sought licenses or endorsements, ODDS can have a direct impact on the
provider agencies involved in abuse and neglect. In previous years, most reports of abuse and neglect involved licensed settings. Employees in these settings are mandatory reporters of abuse and
are trained to recognize signs of abuse. The estimate of abuse for this population increased by 0.33% over last year’s figure. The adult population receiving these services increased 2.0% from 2021
to 2022 (8438 individuals to 8606) and the number of adults with a substantiated abuse allegation rose from 113 in 2021 to 144 in 2022, an increase of 27.4%. Because of a lack of national abuse
data, it is a challenge to establish a meaningful IDD abuse rate target. Targets are currently based on trends of historical data. Oregon’s recent transition to a Centralized Abuse Management (CAM)
information system is providing enhanced tracking and monitoring of abuse referrals and investigations. The CAM system will enhance access and availability of information across the state and
greatly improve the ability to make data informed decisions.
Factors Affecting Results
The COVID-19 pandemic had an impact on every facet of life and undoubtedly impacted the abuse of vulnerable populations for the past several years. While we do not have data that clearly informs
how the pandemic influenced abuse and neglect of IDD populations, a brief search and review of recent findings demonstrated significant challenges to IDD clients. Key impacts included difficulty
understanding restrictions and changes in normal routines, loss of socialization, and often a lower quality of health care. The pandemic increased stress associated with confinement, and in some
cases increased clients’ reliance on abusive support persons while simultaneously creating barriers to reporting. Implementation of emergency infection control measures meant that individuals with
developmental disabilities were more isolated than normal. Such isolation could reduce abuse rates simply by reducing person-to-person interactions. However, the isolation increased stress and
therefore may have increased the potential for negative interactions between IDD clients and their service providers. Decreased staffing may also have reduced the number of potential observers,
resulting in reduced reporting of allegations. Over the previous four years the overall trend has been a decline in abuse rates which reached a low during the first year of the pandemic and has
increased slightly each year since. Even with the increase in the rate the past two years, abuse rates appear below the pre-COVID levels. It appears that, consistent with other trends, unknown COVID
factors had a significant effect on abuse findings and/or reporting for these populations. Additionally, abuse rates for people with intellectual/developmental disabilities can be affected by other factors
including: • The high acuity and service needs of residents being served in community-based care settings. • High turnover rate of treatment and support staff in all settings. • An adult’s right to make
decisions about their living situation, companions, etc. • Barriers to the reporting of abuse by cognitively impaired clients. • Limited resources available to respond to and support people with

Factors Affecting Results
Housing continues to be a paramount concern across Oregon and the lack of available housing and high housing costs are driving rapid increases in housing instability and homelessness.3,4
Federally funded temporary pandemic protections like Oregon Emergency Rental Assistance Program (OERAP), Emergency Housing Vouchers, stimulus payments, and SNAP emergency allotments,
along with policies such as eviction and foreclosure moratoria, helped people stay economically afloat and remain in their homes during the pandemic. In 2023, most of those policies have been rolled
back. The remaining social safety net is inadequate to support long-term housing stability in the face of significant economic turmoil, particularly for people of color and those experiencing generational
poverty. Many people accrued housing debt during the pandemic and currently in Oregon, around 40,000 households still owe nearly $63 million in back rent.5 Layer on inflation and rising housing
costs and we see a recipe for substantially declining housing stability. Eviction rates in Oregon are now higher than they were in pre-pandemic years, and people identifying their race as Black or
Other experience more evictions than all other populations combined.6
Concerns about housing unaffordability were reflected throughout the SSP well-being survey conducted this year. People repeatedly cite that rents are too high, housing costs are increasing, they are
worried about making rent, and are struggling to find help to pay for housing. Based on the survey comments, the phrase “...housing needs are fully met” and the word “stability” may be relative terms
depending on the individual or family’s situation. In some situations, affording housing may not be a concern, but safety and quality of housing are pressing issues.
Over the past five years SSP expanded TANF funded supports to cover housing related emergent needs. SSP has also provided for innovative contracting for housing related services, including
navigation services in partnership with community-based organizations and agencies. SSP continues to partner with Oregon Housing and Community Services and other community-based
organizations to support access to needed housing assistance.

within everyone. Foundational to these changes are intentionally involving families; having a philosophy and practice of being person centered; being strengths based and having resources that build

How Are We Doing
In the Summer 2023, SSP conducted a statewide survey of Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program (SNAP) participants who applied or reapplied for SNAP in 2023. The survey was voluntary and
deployed in six languages: Arabic, Russian, Somali, Spanish, Vietnamese, and English. Of 45,987 SNAP applicants sent the survey link, there were 4,098 survey respondents which represents an
8.9% percent overall response rate.
Based on the survey, 76 percent of respondents reported that they were satisfied with their customer experience at ODHS. This represents a 13.5% drop in customer satisfaction from 2022.
Factors Affecting Results
The adequacy benefits may be a critical factor influencing customer satisfaction. Survey respondents who opted to provide more input consistently express dissatisfaction with benefit amounts that fall
short of covering their basic food needs. The financial strain resulting from inadequate benefits may also negatively affect individuals and families' satisfaction. Respondents reported that financial
strain extended beyond food affordability, impacting housing stability, utility payments, and overall financial security. Lastly, respondents responsible for feeding families and children express
heightened concerns about the impact of inadequate benefits.

How Are We Doing
For reporting year 2023, Asian/Pacific Islanders, Hispanic, and White populations continue to meet or exceed their targets (down/less is good), though there was a slight uptick in disproportionality at
entry for Hispanic populations.  Black/African American children/young adults experienced a significant drop in disproportionality (up/more is bad) towards the proposed target, and so did American
Indian/Alaskan Native children/young adults,  dropping to a lower rate of disproportionality for the reporting year.
Factors Affecting Results
Multiple factors may have contributed directly to the performance of this KPI, including targeted recruitment for the implementation of selected best-practices that support retention and hiring of a
diverse workforce with the goal of reflecting the community served.  The implementation of the Racial Equity and Social Justice Tool (RESJ) that provides an in-depth review of policies and initiatives
that may create racialized outcomes has seen increased use since its development, and the newly created Equity Training and Workforce development program helped target equity training for staff.
Finally, the conducting of case reviews and staffings in partnership with equity experts and the community to lift these themes into CW CQI processes likely played a large part.
