How Are We Doing
Note: this Key Performance Metric sets targets for increasing statewide regular attendance. The data analysis narrative shifted from reporting chronic absenteeism rates to reporting regular attendance
rates as approved by the Legislature and to align with a strengths-based approach.
The regular attender rate for all students increased in 2024-2025 by 0.8 percentage points from the prior year, 1 percentage point for students of color and 1 percentage point for students with
disabilities. While the increase in regular attender rates is to be celebrated, focal student groups still face disproportionately lower rates of regular attendance compared to all students, white students,
or Asian students.
The overall regular attender rate increased by 0.8 percentage points from the prior year.
All grade bands except for Grades 5 and 12 increased modestly from the 2023-24 school year. Grades K-2 increased by 1.8 percentage points from 23-24 to 70.7%. The Kindergarten regular
attender rate increased the most of all grade bands, by 2.5 percentage points. Grades K-7 have regular attender rates greater than the statewide average for all students (66.5%). Grade 12
decreased in regular attender rates by 1 percentage point from 2023-24.
Regular attender rates increased for all students of color except for Asian students. Regular attender rates increased the most for Black/African American students (2.6 percentage points to
actual target
60%) and for American Indian/Alaska Native students (1.3 percentage points to 54.1%). However, rates for both groups remain below the state average.
Regular attender rates increased by 2.7 percentage points from the 23-24 school year to 59.9% for students currently navigating foster care.
While regular attender rates increased for most student groups, only the following student groups exceed the statewide average for all students: Asian students (82.8%), Talented and Gifted
students (80.1%), Military Connected students (71.1%), and white students (69.3%). The regular attender rate for Multi-Racial students also increased to 66.5%, which mirrors the statewide
average for all students (a 1.2 percentage point increase 23-24 to 24-25).
Factors Affecting Results
Students are considered to be a regular attender if they attended school at a rate of more than 90 percent.
Attendance is one of the first indicators of belonging and wellness.
Students who do not
meet this rate are considered to be experiencing chronic absenteeism. This threshold can mask variation within attendance and absence patterns and reasons for missed days. Supporting students’
regular attendance requires a comprehensive and collaborative response, as the numerous factors impacting attendance are often outside of students’ control, with acute and pervasive impacts.
Schools and districts can utilize their local student success systems to identify and address patterns in barriers to attendance, belonging, and engagement.
A complex combination of factors both within the school and external to school can create barriers to regular attendance. Identifying patterns of absences, including absences that are acute or
persistent, and the reasons for the absences helps ensure students and families can access the support they need.
Four categories of root causes include barriers to attendance, negative school
experiences, lack of engagement in school, and misconceptions about the impact of absences on student learning. These challenges can be shaped by broader contextual factors such as student
well-being, school climate, family dynamics, neighborhood conditions, and socio-economic inequities.
Additionally, students’ age, social context, and personal experiences with school can intersect
with root causes.
Oregon Attendance Initiatives
While there are specific programs with dedicated funds to address attendance supports, such as ODE’s Integrated Programs (Student Investment Account, High School Success, Career & Technical
Education, Early Indicator & Intervention Systems, Early Literacy Success School District Grants, Every Day Matters, Federal School Improvement for Comprehensive/Targeted Supports, Career
Connected Learning, Continuous Improvement Planning), attendance is embedded across the agency from transportation to child nutrition to school counseling.
All of ODE’s work addresses root
causes of chronic absenteeism. Tackling the root causes of chronic absenteeism involves the whole education system. Partnerships between school districts, students and families, community-based
organizations, and ESDs are key to developing systems that center students’ sense of self, belonging, and well-being both within schools and the larger community.
One of Oregon’s attendance initiatives is a program called Every Day Matters (EDM), which seeks to expand systemic understanding and support to address attendance barriers and the root causes
of chronic absenteeism. EDM elevates partnerships and root cause understanding through multiple routes:
EDM provides funding to all 19 Education Service Districts (ESDs) to provide a continuum of supports for school districts and charter schools, specifically focusing on student attendance,
belonging, and engagement.
In the 23-25 biennium, EDM awarded 16 grants to fund
community-centered approaches
to addressing the root causes of chronic absenteeism. These grants seek to improve attendance by
building trust and partnership between families, communities, districts, and ESDs to collectively address the barriers to student belonging, engagement, and attendance in school (Henderson,
Mapp, et al. 2016).
EDM's focus on relationships and engagement is scaled up through investments in the
Integrated Programs
, such as High School Success, Career and Technical Education, and the Student
Investment Account. Through the Integrated Programs, many districts centered student engagement through investments in SEL curriculum, hiring additional staff—including school counselors
—to meet the mental and behavioral needs of students, and providing professional development opportunities to staff on trauma-informed practices, culturally responsive practices, and SEL
practices. The EDM team partners with ODE staff to support the alignment of the continuum of supports offered to districts working on myriad aspects related to attendance.
In the 2023-25 Integrated Programs application cycle, the co-development process of Longitudinal Performance Growth Targets provided opportunities for ODE and districts to discuss student
engagement and attendance support systems. Districts engage in semi-annual progress monitoring, which provides ODE another avenue to support districts investments towards regular
attendance and engagement through coaching.
In the 2025-27 Integrated Programs application cycle, districts were required to review
Student Success Plans
- culturally specific, community-directed, and equity-centered statewide education
plans for specific student focal groups, when developing plans for Integrated Programs. The Student Success Plans provide recommendations for student engagement, belonging and
attendance related to specific focal groups and provide actionable steps that schools and districts can take to improve outcomes for students in these groups and for students overall, which can
impact root causes of chronic absenteeism and increase regular attendance.

Tribal Attendance Promising Practices (TAPP) Grant
In 2016, the Oregon legislature set aside $1.5 million to support pilot projects to reduce chronic absenteeism of native students in nine preselected Oregon sites. This project was referred to as the
Tribal Attendance Pilot Project (TAPP), but due to the success of the program, is now known as Tribal Attendance Promising Practices, which now awards $1.9 million to support ten school districts in
addressing the root causes of chronic absenteeism in AI/AN+ students. TAPP funds allow each district to hire a “TAPP Family Advocate”, who intentionally monitors the attendance of all AI/AN+
students. All TAPP districts have one or more Tribal Partners who support them in their TAPP efforts.
Thirty-four total schools across 10 school districts are served directly by a TAPP Family Advocate. These school districts currently serve 42% of the AI/AN student population in Oregon (2,251 AI/AN
and over 6,327 AI/AN+ students). Given that TAPP is intended to share lessons learned from TAPP school sites throughout an entire district K-12 ecosystem, TAPP’s potential to impact the
educational experience of AI/AN+ students is tremendous.
In an analysis of the regular attender data:
TAPP schools had an AI/AN+ regular attender rate average of 51.6%. This is 2.6% below the state AI/AN average of 54.2%.
TAPP districts had an AI/AN+ regular attender rate of 53.4%.
59% of schools served directly by a TAPP Family Advocate exceeded the state AI/AN+ regular attender rate of 54.2%.
TAPP elementary schools had an AI/AN+ regular attender rate average of 60%. TAPP elementary attendance continues to be the strongest of all grade levels.
While the AI/AN+ regular attender rate was lower than desired, 14 schools who did not meet or exceed the state AI/AN+ regular attender rate average had an average Tier 2 regular attender rate of
38%. This means that students attending 80-89% of the time are showing promise. Regular attender rates for AI/AN+ are anticipated to show tremendous gains next school year, as numerous
students are on the cusp of being regular attenders. This reflects the focus of TAPP districts in identifying root causes and implementing culturally appropriate tiered attendance systems.
Lastly, traditional metrics cannot measure the holistic impact TAPP has on Native students. A powerful set of common themes emerged from the end of biennium reflections shared from tribal partners
and school districts:
Family Advocate Leadership:
Advocates are not auxiliary — they are catalysts for relational accountability, family reconnection (both to school and to their culture), and systems change in both
the schools they serve and in their school districts.
Root-Cause-Oriented Practices:
Districts embraced frameworks that named trauma, poverty, racism, and systemic neglect as causes of chronic absenteeism — not student or family failure.
This reflects a powerful shift in our TAPP School Districts to critically examine and identify systemic shifts needed in our organizations to serve Native students, their families, and tribal
communities in more culturally sustaining ways.
Healing-Centered Engagement:
Shifting away from punitive approaches of addressing attendance and towards healing-centered engagement through cultural activities, home visits, and being
mindful of each interaction with students and families as opportunities to restore trust with our schools, with educators, and even the relationships families have with the public school system.
Elevating Tribal Voice
: Fostering tribal partnership efforts and building space for Tribal partners to shape district strategies and policies. From unique attendance codes to honor when Native
students are engaging in cultural events and activities during the school day, to the creation or revision of robust Memorandums of Understanding between the tribe and school district, or in the
implementation of both Tribal History/Shared History lesson plans
AND
the curriculum of the local tribe(s), TAPP school districts are identifying these as foundational practices that must be in
place given the proximity to a Sovereign Nation.
Place-Based Cultural Curriculum:
Expansion of Native language instruction, cultural events, and curriculum shaped by tribes has increased significantly this biennium in our ten TAPP school
districts.
TAPP is essential and a critically important protective factor for American Indian and Alaska Native + students. The impact of the grant funds cannot be found in singular metrics, but from hearing from
tribes directly on what these investments mean for their tribal sovereignty and health of their most valued resource – their youth.

How Are We Doing
Proficiency levels for all groups are well below the established targets, although overall ELA achievement improved for the first time since 2019, rising from 39% to 40.3%. The percentage of 3rd
graders demonstrating proficiency on the statewide summative assessment in English language arts (reading, writing, listening, and research) has been low for almost a decade. The percentage of
3rd graders meeting or exceeding literacy standards has ranged from 48.5% (2016) at its highest to 39% (2024) at its lowest. In response, educators are working diligently to accelerate literacy
learning. Proficiency scores in 2025 (40.3%) have increased slightly from the previous year (39%), indicating that leaders across the state must continue to prioritize literacy initiatives and investments.
It is worth noting that overall 2025 proficiency rates are the highest that they’ve been since the COVID19 pandemic.
There continue to be disparities between the overall percent of students meeting or exceeding literacy standards compared to students of color (22.9%) and students experiencing disabilities (20.9%).
It is important to note, however, that this KPM and its targets only represent students who demonstrated full proficiency (a level 3 “meets” or 4 “exceeds”). When considering all students progressing
toward grade-level knowledge (including the 24% of students scoring a level 2), a majority of 3rd grade students (64%) demonstrated at least some progress on grade-level knowledge and skills. 36%
of students do not yet consistently demonstrate grade-level knowledge and skills in literacy (a level 1). These data points continue to affirm our state-level efforts to support literacy growth through the
Early Literacy Success Initiative grants and the literacy frameworks.

Factors Affecting Results
Oregon must do better for our students and communities, and ODE is facing this challenge with urgency, intention, and strategy. ODE and the State Legislature have made several investments under
the Early Literacy Success Initiative focused on improving outcomes under this KPM. Factors impacting this KPM and efforts to continue improving outcomes are highlighted below.
K-5 Early Literacy Framework
Grounded in evidence-based research,
Oregon’s Early Literacy Framework (OELF)
aims to build coherence, clarity, and shared expectations, while providing districts, schools, educators, and
communities a shared vision for what strong early literacy instruction looks like. Building on the momentum of the publication of the Framework in the spring of 2023, the ODE Literacy Team continues
to promote research-based, culturally and linguistically responsive literacy instruction through presentations at statewide conferences, webinars, and online resources.
Early Literacy Success Initiative
Early Literacy Success School District Grants (ELSSDG)
During the 2023 legislative session, the Oregon Legislature enacted
ORS 327.825 - 327.845
, Oregon’s Early Literacy Success Initiative. After completing the first year (2023-24) of implementation,
data from grantee annual reports showed:
Approximately 80% of school districts, representing about 185,000 students, have adopted research-aligned core curriculum for Early Literacy from the State Board of Education adopted list.
Of the data reported, 94% of grantees reported that most of their teachers (76-100%) have access to the core materials they are required to teach.
Of the data reported, 8,317 Kindergarten through 5th grade teachers in English Language Arts Assignments received early literacy professional development or coaching.
Across the state, 16,901 Kindergarten through 5th grade students participated in high-dosage tutoring and 5,788 students participated in extended learning programming.
In the second year (2024-25) of implementation, districts continued to invest most heavily in professional development and coaching, high-dosage tutoring, and the adoption and implementation of
curriculum and materials. ODE worked to support grantees by creating an
Allowable Use Guide
to outline best practices in each allowable use, providing technical assistance and support to grantees,
and updating administrative rules to better support research-aligned best practices.

allowable uses of the grant. ODE will begin monitoring district improvements in ELA achievement and identify districts where achievement is not improving in alignment with the accountability
initiative.

Integrated Guidance
In the 2025-27 biennium, districts applied for ELSSDG funding through the updated Integrated Guidance application, which brings together federal and state educational investments focused on
innovation and improvement. The purpose of this consolidation was to allow LEAs to more easily blend and braid funds to improve outcomes and learning conditions that better serve their
communities. Additionally, the updated application will help districts focus on literacy as a component of their strategic improvement plans. ODE will understand the highest-leverage actions taken by
districts through the reporting of student growth assessment data, which will influence the types of literacy support ODE provides across the state.
Early Literacy Success Community Grants
To further support early literacy,
ORS 327.825 - 327.845
, Oregon’s Early Literacy Success Initiative, also established funding for the Early Literacy Success Community Grants to strengthen
relationships, communication, and resource sharing between schools, families, and communities. The Early Literacy Success Community grants are competitive grants that establish funding for
eligible Community-Based Organizations’ literacy programs serving children in Pre-K through 3rd grade.
In March of 2025, thirteen (13) Community-Based Organizations were identified as Cohort 1 for implementation of the Early Literacy Success Community grant throughout the 2025-27 biennium. The
awarded grant recipients have begun implementation of their programs in the communities they serve. This investment seeks to improve literacy outcomes through extended-learning opportunities
offered to students and families in their local community.
Early Literacy Success Tribal Grants
ORS 327.825 - 327.845
, Oregon’s Early Literacy Success Initiative, also established funding for the Early Literacy Success Tribal grants as a dedicated investment to strengthen early literacy and
language revitalization in partnership with Oregon’s nine federally recognized Tribes. The goals and metrics of these grants are co-designed and led by Tribes, honoring their educational sovereignty
while ensuring each Tribe leads according to its own priorities and values. This approach draws strength from each Tribe’s unique cultural and community priorities, advancing both literacy outcomes
and cultural connectedness.
In 2025, seven of the nine federally recognized tribes received funding for implementation of the Early Literacy Success Tribal grants throughout the 2025-27 biennium. The federally recognized tribes
have begun implementation of their programs in the communities they serve. This investment hopes to improve literacy outcomes through extended-learning opportunities offered to students and
families in their local community. Through these grants, the Office of Indian Education (OIE) supports Tribes in delivering culturally grounded literacy instruction and revitalizing Tribal languages in
early learning environments. The program also fosters intergenerational education, community engagement, and strengthens the Tribal educator workforce through Indigenous pedagogies and
professional development. Between 2023 and 2025, seven of Oregon’s nine Tribes actively participated in culturally responsive literacy programming, reaching over 1,350 Pre-K–5 students and 1,125
families. The initiative produced 130+ culturally specific resources, supported Tribal language revitalization, trained over 30 Tribal educators, and engaged Elders in storytelling and cultural practices to
strengthen identity and intergenerational learning. OIE will continue offering technical assistance and relational visits to support Tribes in co-designing indicators of success, strengthening Tribal-
district partnerships, and building sustainable language and literacy ecosystems. This work affirms Oregon’s commitment to educational self-determination by reducing reporting burdens; expanding
family and community engagement; and grounding youth in culture, language, and identity through intergenerational learning and healing.
Comprehensive Literacy State Development Grant (CLSD)
In Fall of 2024, Oregon was awarded the Comprehensive Literacy State Development (CLSD) Grant from the U.S. Department of Education. Through CLSD, Oregon is providing $55 million of grant-
in-aid to 30 of Oregon’s highest-need school districts over the next four years. Together, these districts serve more than half of Oregon’s students, including a majority of those identified as Students
with Disabilities, Emergent Bilingual learners, and Students Experiencing Poverty.
The CLSD grant is designed to build educator capacity and expand the use of high-quality, evidence-based literacy practices in high-need schools. While CLSD provides opportunities for districts to
invest in the same allowable uses as the Early Literacy Success School District Grants, it expands the scope to serve students from birth through 12th grade. This historic investment will help ensure
every Oregon student has the opportunity to develop strong literacy skills and succeed in school and beyond.
Dyslexia Policy and Practices
ODE is continuing to support the Dyslexia legislation that was enacted in 2018 by providing a framework for early identification and prevention. ODE monitors district implementation of dyslexia
policies and provides targeted technical assistance. Implementation of the Early Literacy Framework and the programs associated with Oregon’s Early Literacy Success Initiative aim to increase the
capacity of schools and districts to implement systems and practices designed to support the strengths and needs of all students, including students experiencing dyslexia, as they grow in their literacy
learning.
Literacy Professional Learning
ODE continues to provide statewide professional learning on research-based, high-quality literacy instruction and programming aligned with the Early Literacy Framework through conference
presentations, webinars, and workshops. Because high-quality literacy instruction is crucial for secondary students as well, the Literacy Team also promotes the Adolescent Literacy Framework, which
was released in February 2025. In collaboration with WestEd, the Literacy Team is creating resources aligned to the practices within the Adolescent Literacy Framework, which will be published by
October 2025.
Communities of Practice:
In the summer of 2025, ODE collaborated with Stand for Children to host a Summer Learning Community of Practice for district literacy leaders who plan summer
programming. The Community of Practice provided a space to share research-based practices, tackle common challenges and collaborate with other literacy leaders. In October of 2025, ODE
will host a similar Community of Practice focused on High-Dosage Tutoring, which will bring together district and school leaders planning tutoring programs to share practices and understand
what research tells us maximizes impact on student achievement.
Instructional Frameworks Website
: Launched in August 2024, the website houses a variety of free resources for districts & ESDs to support professional learning related to
Oregon’s Early Literacy Framework. Additional resources for both the Early Literacy and Adolescent Frameworks will continue to be added to the Instructional Frameworks
website as they are developed by ODE.
Statewide Literacy Excellence Network (SLEN): ODE established SLEN in October 2024 in collaboration with the Oregon Association of Education Service Districts (OAESD). SLEN
brings together literacy leaders in nineteen ESDs across the state of Oregon on a monthly basis. Through SLEN, ESD leaders learn about Oregon’s Literacy Frameworks and
statewide resources; develop a deeper understanding of effective, research-based literacy instruction; and collaborate to identify aligned strategies for implementation of high-
quality literacy programming in local contexts across the state. SLEN will continue to meet through the 2025-26 school year to align support for districts, ensuring coherence and
consistency in working towards Oregon’s vision for early literacy success.
Literacy Leaders Network:
ODE hosts a monthly Literacy Leaders’ Network (LLN) meeting, a collaboration between the Oregon Council of Teachers of English, the Oregon State Literacy
Association, the Oregon Association of School Libraries, and the Educator Advancement Council. Each month, ODE specialists share assessment data and trends, followed by targeted
professional development for literacy leaders across the state. Meetings consist of high-leverage instructional practices geared towards addressing the opportunities for growth revealed in data.
Supporting Implementation of High-Quality Instructional Materials
Currently, 80% of Oregon districts are using high-quality instructional materials from the State Board of Education’s adopted list. In 2025, Oregon entered into a partnership with the Instructional
Materials Professional Development (IMPD) Network, a group sponsored by the Council of Chief State School Officers (CCSSO). As one of only 15 states in the network, Oregon receives support for
the development and implementation of a strategic plan to significantly increase the use of standards-aligned, high-quality instructional materials across the state, and to ensure that teachers have
access to professional development that is grounded in the use of these materials. The IMPD will also help to reimagine the instructional materials adoption process to ensure coherence and ease of
implementation.
Small, Rural School Library Revitalization Grant
In July 2024, ODE was awarded funding through the Congressionally Funded Community Projects (CFCP) Fund. With these funds, ODE provided small grants to 35 small and rural K-5 school
districts to revitalize and diversify school library collections, modernize furnishings, and support professional development of library staff, all towards the goal of improving student achievement. All
grantees were also recipients of the ESSER III School Library Revitalization Grant, allowing them to utilize funding in unique ways to make their library spaces the hub of their school
communities.Districts built reading programs and incentives that promote habits of reading for their schools and communities.

How Are We Doing
2024-25 was a year of continued growth for 9th grade students on track to graduate, surpassing pre-pandemic levels. This increase is a testament to the hard work and innovative practices of
Oregon’s educators and students. The rate for students of color (Black, Hispanic, American Indian/Alaska Native, and Native Hawaiian/Pacific Islander students) grew more than the overall rate, 3.0
percentage points versus 1.8 percentage points, representing progress towards gap closure, though work remains to be done to ensure that all students are on-track to graduate at the end of their 9th
grade year. The on-track rate for students with disabilities increased at the same rate as all students, indicating that additional focus and support are needed to help districts serve these students in
ways that are culturally-sustaining, equitable, and designed to help identify and remove barriers.

reviewing data, identifying barriers and interventions, and conducting engagement and outreach to connect with all of their students and families/caregivers. Additionally, over one quarter of districts
reported more than 90% of their ninth grade students being on track to graduate in four years. Districts and schools will continue to maintain rigorous expectations and support to
accelerate learning
for all students in order to ensure all students are on track to graduate.

The statewide graduation rate for the class of 2024 is 81.8 percent. Although the increase in overall graduation rates are relatively slight, some student populations have experienced larger gains. Key
findings from the data include:
Students completing two credits in an approved Career and Technical Education (CTE)  Program of Study (CTE Concentrators) significantly exceeded the statewide average, graduating at a

How Are We Doing
Because the Extended Participation Rate tracks participation within 12 months of high school graduation, the most recent year for which data is available is for the high school graduating class of
2022-23. For the class of 2022-23, the Extended Participation Rate for Oregon students was 54.5%, an increase of 1.2 percentage points from  the class of 2021-22. This rate is not comparable to
rates prior to 2021-22 due to a change in the methodology used to calculate the Extended Participation Rate. (Note: To better meet Federal reporting requirements, Oregon changed the methodology
used to calculate Extended Participation Rates beginning with the Class of 2022. The denominator used for the rate changed from including graduates from Oregon’s 4-year Graduation Cohort to
focusing on students who graduated in the same academic year (regardless of when they began high school). The numerator used for the rate changed from looking at college enrollment within 16
months of graduation to college enrollment within 12 months of graduation.)The Extended Participation Rate currently sits more than 20 percentage points below the target.
Factors Affecting Results
A number of factors affect the college participation and success of Oregon high school graduates.  Principal among them is the quality of preparation that students receive in the early grades and in
high school.  A number of other factors, however, also affect the rate at which students enter college and the success they have there, including the impact students’ financial and family circumstances
has on their ability to attend college and to remain there once they start. In addition, college attendance rates are sensitive to labor market conditions, with fewer students enrolling in college when job
prospects are better in a growing economy. Nationally, first-time college student enrollment has been down since at least the fall of 2016 (National Student Clearinghouse). Enrollment of first-time
freshman college students decreased by 2 percent each fall term from 2016 through 2017, followed by a 4 percent drop in fall 2018 and an additional 2 percent drop in fall 2019. Due to the Covid-19
pandemic, enrollment dropped by nearly 10 percent in fall 2020. By Fall 2021, enrollment had nearly stabilized before increasing by 4 percent in fall 2022 (
National Student Clearinghouse, 2023
