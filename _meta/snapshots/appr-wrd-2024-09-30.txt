How Are We Doing
During the 2024 reporting period, 26 percent of the 342 targeted high priority watersheds had flows added for fish.  The percentage matches performance from the prior reporting year.  The amount of
water placed instream can fluctuate from year-to-year based on water user interest in leasing water instream and the location of water rights proposed for instream lease.  Since this KPM was created
in 2002, the Department has permanently protected a total of 1,545.92 cubic feet per second (cfs) of water instream. This total is comprised of the following: 1) instream transfers at 422.38 cfs; 2)
allocations of conserved water at 265.54 cfs; and 3) converted hydroelectric rights at 858.0 cfs.  An additional 5,008.93 cfs was protected instream through temporary instream leases in 2023. Six of
those leases were power rights that protected 4,376.52 cfs instream.
Factors Affecting Results
The 2024 reporting period was the calendar year 2023 period.  Streamflow restoration efforts depend largely on the voluntary actions of water right holders to place water instream. Success on this
measure relies on the hard work of our conservation partners, our staff, and a general increased comfort level among water users with dedicating water instream . Streamflow restoration benefits from
well-established, active conservation partners with 39 percent of Oregon's flow restoration transactions involve a third party such as The Freshwater Trust, Deschutes River Conservancy, or Trout
Unlimited; while the remaining 61 percent of flow restoration activities occur directly between the water right holder and the Department.

How Are We Doing
In calendar year 2023, staff reported a total of 8,726 regulatory actions. Regulatory actions are actions by staff that cause a change in water use behavior. Of the 8,726 total regulatory actions taken in
2023, 2,941 – 33.7 percent of the total – were conducted to regulate for instream water rights. The report year target is 50 percent, which was not met.  While the target was not met it was due in large
part to not needing to regulate for some instream water rights as water conditions were more favorable to the established instream water rights.
Watermasters and assistant watermasters are responsible for monitoring and protecting instream water rights.  These staff members are also assisted by regional hydrographic technicians who
conduct numerous streamflow measurements in support of instream water right monitoring.  Watermasters report monitoring activities and regulatory actions taken each calendar year for each stream
into the Field Activity Database (FAD) for ongoing tracking and enforcement purposes.
Factors Affecting Results
Instream water rights are often junior to other surface water rights and are actively monitored by the Water Resources Department.  Many stream flows with instream water rights are met naturally
throughout the season which do not require regulation on their behalf. In years with well-timed precipitation and high stream flows, the total number of streams regulated is likely to go down. In years
with lower stream flows the total number of streams regulated is likely to go up because of the greater demand and less supply for all beneficial uses. The number of streams regulated varies with the
amount and timing of rainfall and snowmelt run off in any given year, temperatures, as well as availability of staff resources. This KPM is specific to a regulation for instream water rights.  In addition,
actions taken by field staff to address unauthorized uses of water benefit instream needs even if not reflected by this particular metric.

How Are We Doing
In 2023, watermasters and assistant watermasters reported 8,726 regulatory actions. Field staff also reported 29,304 compliance checks to determine if water right holders were complying with the law
or a regulatory action. Of the 29,304 compliance checks, 99 percent of the time water use was consistent with regulatory orders or Oregon water laws.  With approximately 89,500 water rights
statewide and several hundred thousand exempt use wells, plus an unknown number of unauthorized uses, it is not possible for Department staff to assess overall compliance with state water law
each year.
Factors Affecting Results
The Department has utilized the Field Activities Database (FAD) since 2018.  Ongoing refinement to this database tool, clarifications in data entry and ongoing quality control and assurance have led
to improvements in capturing necessary metrics for work done by department staff. As the Field Services Division has grown substantially due to legislative investments in recent years, the ability to
address reported issues and complaints in a timely fashion has improved. The investments have also allowed for the Department to be more forward-looking and proactively investigate water use via
staff-initiated investigations. This is reflected in the number of community-based complaints continuing a downward trend of 377, which is down from the previous years of 508 in 2022 and again down
from 1,120 in 2021. In contrast, the number of field staff initiated investigations rose in 2023 with 1,142 actions, up from 894 in 2022 and 732 in 2021.
As part of the regulatory actions, 162 notices of violation (NOV) were issued in calendar year 2023. This is a slight increase from the previous year in which the Department issued 153 NOVs. Both the
regulated and unregulated cannabis industry accounts for a large number of the sites where NOV’s are issued. However, the Department’s added staffing capacity also allows for proactively visiting
and auditing sites for water law compliance, resulting in the issuance of NOVs around the state when violations are observed. The newly formed Enforcement Section (ES) began streamlining
enforcement processes in 2022 and continues to assist in enforcement efficiencies to include better tracking of enforcement referrals leading to issuance of orders and regulatory notices.  In addition
to the above NOV totals, the ES assisted field staff with the issuance of 108 measurement device notices and issued 16 notices of assessment for civil penalties totaling over $52,000. The Department
may not collect all of these assessments as some may be reduced as part of stipulated settlement agreements or they remain part of ongoing casework. The increased investments in field and
enforcement staff are improving water use compliance across the state.

How Are We Doing
During the reporting period, the Department added one gage, and discontinued one gage, with no net change in the count of stream flow gages. The Department operated a total of 251 gages during
this period, a 17 percent increase over the 2001 benchmark (215 gaging stations) but remains under the targeted 25 percent goal or approximately 269 gages. The Department continues to participate
in the shared statewide gaging network which increases the capacity of the Department and other water partners to monitor data from measurement gages beyond what our agency is responsible for
maintaining.
Factors Affecting Results
During 2020 and 2021, the COVID pandemic caused funding and staffing challenges, along with some extreme flooding events, leading to a decrease in the size of our gage network. In 2022, the
Department spent $1 million one-time ARPA dollars to purchase equipment for new and existing gages.  Most new gages supported basin studies and state-wide investigations, however, most of
these funds were used to replace aging equipment at existing gages. The 2023 Legislature approved additional funding and positions for the Department’s expansion of the gage network in support of
the Surface Water Availability update. Staff are currently working on installing these gages and anticipate expanding the gage network by 10-15 gages by the end of 2025.
Continuing to increase the
size of the gaging network requires the Department to have ongoing and consistent funding for sufficient equipment, hydrographers, and field staff to provide ongoing station operation and
maintenance, and quality assurance and quality control of the data gathered from the statewide gage network.

How Are We Doing
During the current reporting cycle, the Department staff routinely monitored 420 wells in the State Observation Well Network, compared to 419 in the 2022 report. This is still well above the current
target of 15%. Wells in the Observation Well Network produce long standing and high-quality aquifer water level data across the state and are meaningful to the Department’s work.
Although not included in the metric, the Department also measures or receives data from 2,492 additional “observation wells”. These data are used to regularly monitor groundwater levels for special
projects, monitor groundwater administrative areas, provide measurements to satisfy groundwater permit conditions, and support groundwater level data collection for background and special project
area monitoring. These additional observation wells include 816 wells which were measured 1,678 times by Department Staff. Of those wells, 169 are equipped with automated data-logging pressure
transducers that collect water level data several times per day and produce high-frequency time-series data sets. The Department also receives data from licensed professionals who collected 1,803
water level measurements from another 1,387 wells as required by various water right permit conditions. The Department archives and provides these and other groundwater related data via the
Groundwater Information System database and web interface, providing this information directly to public.
Factors Affecting Results
The percentage of monitored wells has increased as the Department added new wells to the State Observation Well Network and replaced others that had previously been dropped off the network.
Since 2013, the Legislature has included dedicated funding for observation wells in the Department's budget, though the total budget has varied from biennium to biennium. Apart from the 23 new
observation wells drilled by the Department in the last decade, State Observation Wells are largely owned by private and other public entities. This can create long-term access issues to wells, as the
Department must rely on private well owners for continued participation in the network and the ongoing reliability and maintenance provided by those outside parties. When the Department loses
access to an observation well, the Department tries to find or drill a suitable replacement well in the same general area targeting the same aquifer, but this process takes time. As a result, the number
and location of State Observation Wells varies from year to year.

How Are We Doing
Work on the Significant Points of Diversion (SigPODs) began in 2000. Working directly with landowners, there have been 1,123 measuring devices installed on SigPODs across the state. Eight
additional devices were installed in the 2023 calendar year through the coordinated efforts of watermasters and assistant watermasters in the Field Services Division (FSD). In addition to the
measurement devices installed on SigPODs, staff have evaluated in the field another 709 significant diversions that are currently not in use. This number changes as a water user may go several
years without using water and then irrigates for a season to reportedly protect the water right from forfeiture. Initiating use would require the installation of a measuring device. Approximately 482 of the
original 2,385 significant diversions still need measuring devices installed.
Factors Affecting Results
The timing of data submittal and the data entry by staff may result in small changes from year-to-year on previously reported numbers. This KPM does not account for additional measuring devices
installed because of other Department actions. The Department focuses measuring device installation where there is noncompliance, complaints, or suspicion of unauthorized use, or in the case of
groundwater, where the Department is conducting scientific research and measurement is needed. This KPM does not track and report on that work, as most of those points of diversion are not
deemed significant points of diversion.
While the FSD has experienced an increase in staff over the past several years, the division has seen staff turnover, particularly for assistant watermasters. Assistant watermasters are often used for
developmental opportunities where staff promote internally or to other agencies after gaining valuable field experience. The FSD also faces high complaint volumes of unauthorized water use, ongoing
drought and subsequent regulation which all increase regulatory workloads on staff; this limits progress on this KPM. The installation of measuring devices typically occurs outside the irrigation season
and is weather dependent, particularly in freezing conditions. Significant outreach and education are needed to help landowners with measuring device installations. Success with measuring device
installation is directly related to time spent by Department field staff, primarily watermasters and assistant watermasters, working with landowners and consultants.

How Are We Doing
For the 16 water management and conservation plans received by the Department between July 1, 2023, and June 30, 2024, 100 percent of the plans were reviewed within the 90-day goal. This is a

are in the process of developing a new or updated water management and conservation plan. These actions demonstrate the Department’s continued efforts to improve water management and
conservation in the state.

How Are We Doing
Cumulatively, the Department issued an Initial Review within 45 days of application filing for 35 percent of water right applications. This initially appears to be a decrease over the last reporting period;
however, a closer look at the data reveals an
increase
in productivity and timeliness across both the Water Rights Section and the Groundwater Section.
Factors Affecting Results
The Water Rights Section issued slightly more Initial Reviews in the current reporting period than the previous reporting period. The Section timely issued an Initial Review for every storage application
and every surface water application. The numbers in the database reflect less than 100% for storage and surface water applications (92% and 82%, respectively) because the Section issued
superseding (replacement) Initial Reviews to account for an inclement weather office closure and issued an Initial Review on a backlog application received in a previous reporting period. In other
words, the Section met the 45-day timeframe for every possible storage and surface water application received within the reporting period. In the previous reporting period, the Water Rights Section
issued only 9 Initial Reviews on groundwater applications, compared to 49 Initial Reviews on groundwater applications within the current reporting period. This substantial increase is due to an
increase in technical reviews completed by the Department’s Groundwater Section. Because groundwater applications often take more than 45 days for the groundwater review, which must occur
before the Water Rights Section can begin to draft the Initial Review, every groundwater application Initial Review released lowers the overall KPM number. Groundwater application Initial Reviews
account for a far higher percentage of the Initial Reviews issued this reporting period (44% versus 8% last reporting period), again lowering the cumulative KPM number. Despite this, the Department
is committed to continuing to release Initial Reviews on groundwater applications as soon as possible.

How Are We Doing
A total of 165 transfer final orders were issued during the time-period July 1, 2023, through June 30, 2024, 19 of which were issued within 120 days of the transfer application being filed. The
Department remains focused on the KPM target even as the workload of transfer applications remains high. Staffing resources added to the Transfer Section during the prior reporting period focused
on producing more work to reduce the number of pending applications. However, with only one senior level staff available to address ongoing training for new staff and to perform final review of draft
documents for accuracy and consistency, the increased production resulting from the additional staff shifted the processing bottleneck from applications awaiting caseworker review to more draft
documents awaiting final review by the senior level lead staff person. In early 2024, the duties of two additional senior level staff in the section were redirected to help with final review. The number of
pending applications reported at the end of FY2023 on July 1, 2023, was 370. It was recently discovered, however, that erroneous reporting methods used in past reporting periods under-reported the
actual number of pending applications. The workload was 448 as of July 1, 2023. Using corrected reporting methods, the number of pending applications at the end of this reporting period on July 1,
2024, increased to 497. The Department’s workload goal is focused on reducing the number of pending applications to 250 or less, at which point staff will be better able to take on processing of new
applications as soon as they are filed.  The Department expects activity with water right transfers to increase over time as users seek changes and more flexibility with their rights.
Factors Affecting Results
Success in meeting this KPM is generally met by the Department’s timely processing of temporary transfer applications, district temporary transfer applications, and drought transfer applications, all of
which can be processed more quickly than standard permanent transfer applications, instream transfers, and groundwater registration modifications. The standard permanent transfer processes
require a 3-step process (
issuance of draft preliminary determinations (DPD), preliminary determinations (PD), and final orders (FO)
) as compared to the temporary and drought transfer process which
is a 1-step process that goes straight to issuance of the FO.
Standard permanent transfer process and waiting times required in rule and statute total 105 days – this figure does not factor in any time necessary for evaluating an application, awaiting completion
of injury reviews from the Watermaster and Groundwater Section hydrogeologists, preparing the required documents (DPD, PD, public notice, and FO), completing data entry and other administrative
processing tasks, or awaiting responses from the applicant. It is difficult for staff to meet this KPM when processing standard permanent transfer processes. In addition, there is continued utilization of
the District Temporary Transfer Pilot Project, which does not require processing of the transfers by Salem staff and significantly reduced the number of easy-to-process temporary district transfer
applications, further limiting the Department’s ability to meet this KPM.
In many parts of the state, significant time is required by the Department’s hydrogeologist staff to review groundwater transfer applications because of the technical nature of groundwater injury
reviews. Furthermore, due to the recent rule changes to groundwater allocation, Groundwater Section staff focused more time on application reviews for new groundwater rights over groundwater right
transfer applications.

How Are We Doing
This customer service survey is currently conducted every even year. According to the survey results, customer satisfaction improved in five of the six categories between 2022 and 2024. Overall
satisfaction increased, with sixty-seven percent of respondents rating their satisfaction with the agency’s customer service as “good” or “excellent” in the 2023-2024 survey period compared to sixty

Survey respondents were further requested to identify if they ultimately received the decision or response they were requesting. Eighty-seven percent of the respondents affirmed they did receive a

Factors Affecting Results
The improved customer experience in the Timeliness category may be attributed to funding that enabled the Water Right Services Division to hire limited duration staff; a temporary increase in funding
to the protest program; records management adjustments; and improvements to the drought application review process. The Legislature approved use of American Rescue Plan Act funds that
enabled the Division to hire limited duration caseworkers to process applications. These limited duration positions ended March 31, 2024. However, these positions likely increased customer
satisfaction in the Timeliness category by enabling the Division to reduce its backlogs for water right applications, extension of time applications, and claims of beneficial use. The Legislature also
provided general funds for the 2021-2023 biennium enabling the Division to resolve a greater number of protests than recent, previous biennia. During the reporting period, the agency adjusted
management and tracking of paper files to improve public records requests response timelines. The Division also improved procedures related to drought transfer applications and emergency water
use applications to reduce processing timelines.
The Division established expectations for all Division staff regarding production metrics and providing timely responses to customer inquiries, which likely increased customer satisfaction in the
Timeliness and Helpfulness categories. Improvements in customer satisfaction in the Availability of Information category may be attributed, in part, to the Division adding a Frequently Asked Questions
section to our website, providing a Notice to Permittee with each water right permit to answer potential permittee questions, updating guidance to applicants to better explain mitigation requirements,
and adding more detailed explanations to agency decision documents. Our improved score in the Accuracy category may be attributed to more rigorous peer review processes, template updates, and
updating staff desk manuals.
