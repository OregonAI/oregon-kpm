How Are We Doing
The FY 2016 result shows an 81 percent compliance rate of refusing sales to minors which misses the legislative target of 82 percent compliance.   The compliance rate was approximately equal to
FY 2014 and FY 2015 each of which also had an 81 percent compliance rate.
The Portland and Salem metropolitan regions experienced 80 percent compliance rates, while the Eugene, Bend and Medford regions ranged between 81 and 89 percent compliance.  Metro
Portland and Salem provided 70 percent of the cases.
The Oregon Liquor Control Commission conducted 933 operations in FY 2016, approximately 700 fewer operations than FY 2015, primarily because of limited staff in the field to conduct both
alcohol compliance operations and implement recreational marijuana licensing,
Factors Affecting Results
A key factor driving these results is frequency of operations. The "perception of detection" is a significant motivator to comply with liquor laws for licensees and their staff.  When the number of
operations decreases, a licensee may not perceive the risk of detection as likely and choose to make decisions that do not comply with the public safety laws, such as selling alcoholic beverages to
minors. Studies of law enforcement practices indicate that a consistent perceived risk of detection and sanctions is a more effective deterrent than inconsistent enforcement with large sanctions.
A related factor affecting results is the random sampling of minor decoy operations.   With the exception of some targeted premises that have committed a prior offense, the majority of operations
are performed on a different group of randomly selected licensee each year. This can result in some variation from past years because now fewer establishments with a recent citation are revisited
within a short time period.

How Are We Doing
The FY 2016 second violation rate is 11.6 percent which is a slight drop from the previous year’s second violation rate of 13.9 percent.   The second violation rate for the last three fiscal years has
been between 11 and 14 percent.  The historical data to FY 2004 produces an average second violation rate of about 16 percent.
During FY 2016, there were 291 licensed premises with at least one serious violation with 357 violations altogether.  This represents about 2.5 percent of total licensed premises for the year.  In FY
2015, there were 391 premises with a serious violation, which constituted about 3 percent of the total premises licensed in the state for that year.  27 premises had with repeat violations in separate
incidents during FY 2016.  65 premises had violations in the first or second year before fiscal year 2016 and were about 0.5% of the state total.
Factors Affecting Results
Total violations dropped for the second year in a row.  From 617 in FY 2014 to 357 in FY 2016. The drop in overall compliance actions reduces the likelihood of any licensee to receive multiple
violations during the period.
During FY 2016, the Oregon Liquor Control Commission experienced significant changes in staffing and business processes as the Commission began to implement the requirements of Measure
91 and House Bill 3400.  Staff spent less time in the field than in past years.  Field time was reduced because the Commission provided additional training and mentoring to new and existing Liquor
Regulatory Specialists and LRS focused on licensing marijuana producers and processors. Additionally a number of experienced inspectors moved into leadership roles within the Commission which
left open LRS positions.   The Oregon Liquor Control Commission ended the fiscal year with 25% of its Liquor Regulatory Specialist Positions unfilled and
Compliance staff have also continued implementing a strategy of using resources to engage with businesses proactively and reserve compliance actions for the more serious violations such as
sales to minors.   The overall number of violations has decreased, but the number of violations for sales to minors has stayed high relative to other violations.

How Are We Doing
The FY 2016 average licensing time for alcohol statewide was 78 days. This is above the legislatively set target of 75 and FY 2015 licensing time of 72 days.  The average time to license for FY
2016 is below the old target of 90 days.  The Commission continues to adapt in order to implement the recreational marijuana program and provide prompt service to alcohol licensees.
Factors Affecting Results
There are many factors affecting the number of days it takes to issue a liquor license; some internal and some external. Internal factors continue to be identified and streamlined through process
improvements and technological solutions (automation). The recent implementation of the marijuana program has caused a number of vacancies in alcohol license investigator positions during
fiscal year 2016.
External factors are difficult to control. Another primary external factor affecting how quickly a liquor license can be issued is the license application review by the local governing body (city or
county). Statute gives local government up to 90 days (45 days plus an additional 45 day extension – if requested) to review a license application within their jurisdiction and provide a
recommendation (positive, negative, or neutral). The OLCC cannot complete the processing of an application until the local government review is completed. Lengthy application review by local
governments usually occurs in the larger metropolitan areas, such as Portland. These areas also have higher numbers of license applications, in absolute terms, which influence the overall
statewide average licensing times. Additionally, the timeliness of the applicant in providing materials necessary to the application investigation can impact overall processing time. Applicants not
prepared for or committed to the process may have longer processing times.  A 2011 analysis found that staff processing time totaled to an average of only 32.9 days per license; only 36% of the
total time to issue a license. The remaining 64% of the time was driven be external factors described above and are outside the control of the agency.
The 2016 results of this measure indicate that the OLCC is above the current target set by the legislature, but is implementing the strategy for resuming improvement. Staffing gaps caused by
implementing the marijuana program are a one-time impact on the time to license measure. The OLCC is pursuing other solutions to its business needs that include regulatory innovations, the
implementation of streamlining measures, and the development of an enterprise-level licensing system that will unite many disconnected processes and increase the agency’s online service
capacity. With these enhancements and innovations the OLCC will resume exceeding the target of this measure.

How Are We Doing
The overall agency rating was 81 percent (rated as good or excellent) compared to 77 percent in 2015. The OLCC missed the 85 percent target in all five areas when weighted averages were taken
over all stakeholder groups.  However, the OLCC met or exceeded the 85 percent average target for surveys from liquor licensees with 94% reporting Very Good or good service.  Liquor agents
and Marijuana license applicants respectively provided an evaluation of 82 and 83 percent extremely or very good service.  Overall, the OLCC exceeded targets in 12 out 30 response categories.
The lower average scores were driven by the much larger and less favorable responses from the general public compared to prior years.  The agency continues to make efforts to increase
information availability through Gov Alerts and posting updates on the agency website.
The total survey response was large with 857 responses in 6 stakeholder groups.
Factors Affecting Results
There was a total of 857 respondents from the five survey groups.  An average of 805 responses for each question. There was a significant difference in responses between alcohol and marijuana
licensees or agents with business before commission provided a much higher evaluation of the Commission than did the public survey.  The increase of general public attnetion to the Oregon
Liquor Control Commission and the widespread confusion that the Commission is responsible for legislation and rules implementing all aspects of marijuana (medical and recreational) may have
contributed to the low evaluations from the public stakeholder group.
Results from the public survey from respondents who have indicated marijuana or distilled spirits as their specific concern, provided a significantly lower evaluation of the OLCC than those who’d
indicated alcohol licensing or regulation as their area of focus. The passage of Measure 91 and OLCC’s role in recreational marijuana resulted in significant public outreach and media coverage,
half of the 434 respondents indicated specific interest in marijuana issues.

How Are We Doing
The OLCC rate of return in FY 2016 was $2.51 for every dollar spent.  It declined from $2.83 in FY 2015 despite a 5% increase in gross merchandising profit.   This is the first time since 2008 that
OLCC missed the target of $2.70.  The decline was caused by a number of one-time expenses for capital improvements and uncompensated start-up expenses from the marijuana program.
During FY 2016 the bottle surcharge generated $16.2 million in additional revenue.
Factors Affecting Results
There are many factors that affect the agency's rate of return. Gross revenue from liquor sales increased 5 percent during FY 2016 over FY 2015, despite this ROI decreased to $2.51.
Three non-recurring expenses account for 99% of the decline in ROI.  An expenditure of $4.9 million was invested in a new high-throughput conveyor system in the Warehouse.   $3.4 million was
used to cover the costs of operating the marijuana program, to be repaid in the next biennium. Capital outlay increased $1.7 million primarily for roof replacement to the two warehouses and OLCC
offices. Agent compensation also rose $3.3 million in FY 2016.
Other factors affecting the result are changes in consumer preferences, resource capacity of the agency’s Distilled Spirits Program, moderating agency policies, budget limitations, etc
.

How Are We Doing
This is the ninth year the self-assessment has been taken by the agency’s Commissioners.  Four Commissioners responded to the FY 2014 request to complete this self-assessment and
all four Commissioners answered every question. There was 100 percent agreement among the responding Commissioners that OLCC best practices were being met. The assessment
indicated that the Commission’s governance practices are hitting the target of 100 percent.
Factors Affecting Results
Response rates will impact the average. In the case of FY 2016 four out of five Commissioners responded to the survey. Communication between the Commission Executives and the
Commission has been robust during the implementation of recreational marijuana, the expansion of liquor stores and investment in the warehouses and operations.  It appears that the
Commissioners felt prepared to respond.  We did have one Commissioner who failed to respond.
